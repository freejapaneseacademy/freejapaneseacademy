## A welcome desk with kanji profile cards

**Can-do:** Introduce yourself and identify another person's basic details. Imagine arriving at a fictional Japanese language-school welcome event. A registration assistant hands you a profile card. The same familiar words from L02 now appear in kanji. You do **not** need to memorize every possible reading of a character today: learn the reading of each **whole word in context**.

> 名前（なまえ）：田中（たなか）  
> 国（くに）：日本（にほん）  
> 仕事（しごと）：先生（せんせい）

This card states that Tanaka's name is Tanaka, their country is Japan, and their occupation is teacher. It does not tell us Tanaka's age, citizenship documentation or place of employment. Names and profiles here are fictional.

## 1. Notice the whole word before individual characters

| Written word | Kana reading | Meaning in this lesson | Notice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 名前 | なまえ | name | 名 and 前 form one everyday word |
| 国 | くに | country | a one-character profile label |
| 日本 | にほん | Japan | 日 + 本 in this country name |
| 人 | ひと | person | standalone word; different reading in 日本人 |
| 日本人 | にほんじん | Japanese person / nationality | 人 is read じん as a suffix here |
| 学生 | がくせい | student | 学 + 生 |
| 先生 | せんせい | teacher / respectful address | 先 + 生 |
| 会社員 | かいしゃいん | company employee | three-character occupational noun |
| 仕事 | しごと | work, job | a useful field heading |
| 私 | わたし | I | often omitted if the subject is clear |

These are **contextual word readings**, not a complete list of readings for each kanji. In particular, **人** can be **ひと** alone and **じん** in **日本人**. Reading 日本人 as *にほんひと* would be incorrect. The last vowel of 先生 is written **い**: せんせい, not せんせ.

## 2. Three contrasts that prevent common mistakes

**Country vs person:** 日本（にほん） names Japan; 日本人（にほんじん） describes a Japanese person or nationality. Adding 人 changes the word. Do not assume someone's nationality merely from their name or where they live.

**Student vs teacher:** 学生（がくせい） means student; 先生（せんせい） means teacher and is also used respectfully to address certain professionals. They share the character 生, but **the whole words have different readings**. You cannot safely read 生 the same way in every compound.

**Kanji vs katakana:** アメリカ is normally written in katakana, not with a new kanji spelling here. The expression アメリカ人（アメリカじん） combines a katakana country name and the kanji 人. Mixed scripts are normal Japanese.

## 3. Read a small first-meeting exchange

> A: 田中（たなか）さんは先生（せんせい）ですか。  
> B: はい、先生です。  
> A: 私（わたし）は学生（がくせい）です。よろしくおねがいします。

**Meaning:** A asks, “Is Tanaka a teacher?” B answers, “Yes, (Tanaka) is a teacher.” A says, “I am a student. Nice to meet you.” In the first sentence, **は** marks the topic and is pronounced *wa*. The final **か** makes a polite yes/no question. **です** makes the nominal statement polite. A's occupation is student; B's answer confirms Tanaka's occupation. The dialogue does not specify anyone's age.

The surname 田中（たなか） is introduced as a **supported name reading**, not a requirement to generalize its individual kanji. Japanese personal names can have multiple readings; verify an unfamiliar person's preferred reading instead of guessing.

## 4. Controlled → guided → independent practice

**Controlled:** Cover the kana column in the table. Point to 学生, 先生 and 日本人. Say or write the *whole-word* readings. Uncover and correct any missed long vowel or final い.

**Guided:** Read this original fictional card and answer in English first:

> 名前：エマ  
> 国：アメリカ  
> 仕事：学生

The card says Emma is a student and lists the United States as her country. It does **not** explicitly say “アメリカ人,” so avoid treating the country field as proof of nationality. Try rewriting the field labels in kana: **なまえ / くに / しごと**.

**Independent:** Write or type two polite statements with the word bank. For example:

> 私は学生です。  
> 田中さんは先生です。

Read them as **わたしは がくせいです。** and **たなかさんは せんせいです。** Compare your work against the model: did you preserve は, choose the right occupation word, and end with です? This ungraded composition is for practice; the scored recognition quiz below assesses reading and meaning, not handwriting quality.
