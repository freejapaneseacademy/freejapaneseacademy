## A message with one tiny but important difference

**Chapter Can-do:** recognize long vowels, small <span lang="ja">っ</span>, and the nasal mora <span lang="ja">ん</span>. This word-study step teaches **meaning in context** and written rhythm cues. It does not grade your hearing or pronunciation: those require actual recordings in a later listening lesson.

Imagine Emi is helping Ren label a family album and a stationery drawer. Ren writes <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> under a photograph of his grandmother. Emi asks him to check the label: he meant <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span>. The extra <span lang="ja">あ</span> matters. Later, Emi asks for <span lang="ja">きって</span> (a postage stamp), not <span lang="ja">きて</span> (the request “come!”). A small character can change the word or even the kind of expression.

The Japanese below is written mainly in hiragana for Level 0. Spaces in teaching examples separate words for beginners; ordinary Japanese normally does not use these spaces.

## 1. Long vowels: a longer beat can change the person

| Japanese | Temporary reading cue | Meaning and use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> | obasan · お・ば・さ・ん (4 mora) | aunt; also a way of referring to a middle-aged woman, depending on context |
| <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> | obaasan · お・ば・あ・さ・ん (5 mora) | grandmother; also an elderly woman |
| <span lang="ja">おじさん</span> | ojisan · お・じ・さ・ん (4 mora) | uncle; also a middle-aged man |
| <span lang="ja">おじいさん</span> | ojiisan · お・じ・い・さ・ん (5 mora) | grandfather; also an elderly man |

In these pairs the extra vowel extends the timing. The distinction is **not** merely a different English spelling. The family terms also have broader uses, so do not assume everyone addressed with these words is literally related to the speaker. Referring to strangers by apparent age can be impolite; here the words are labels in a family album.

**Original example:** <span lang="ja">これは わたしの おばあさん です。</span> — “This is my grandmother.” The phrase is a simple supported album caption. Compare <span lang="ja">これは わたしの おばさん です。</span> — “This is my aunt.” Only the family term changes, but the referent changes too.

**Common mistake:** treating the second <span lang="ja">あ</span> in <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> as optional. It is part of the word. In kana-based counting, each full-sized vowel kana takes one mora here.

## 2. Small っ: a consonant held for one beat

| Japanese | Written timing | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">きて</span> | き・て (2 mora) | “Come!” — the casual request form of くる |
| <span lang="ja">きって</span> | き・っ・て (3 mora) | postage stamp; also written 切手 |
| <span lang="ja">さか</span> | さ・か (2 mora) | slope, hill |
| <span lang="ja">さっか</span> | さ・っ・か (3 mora) | writer, author; also written 作家 |

The small <span lang="ja">っ</span> is **not pronounced as an independent “tsu” syllable** in these words. It marks a brief closure or hold before the following consonant, and counts as one mora. In romaji you may see the following consonant doubled, as in *kitte* and *sakka*. The actual sound is not two fully articulated consonants.

**Original example:** <span lang="ja">きってを ください。</span> — “A postage stamp, please.” This is a short polite request in a shop or post office. Compare <span lang="ja">ここに きて。</span> — “Come here.” This is a casual request to someone familiar. The words do not share a meaning just because their kana are similar.

**Common mistake:** typing <span lang="ja">きて</span> when you mean a stamp. In Japanese input methods, *kitte* commonly converts to <span lang="ja">きって</span>, but check the actual kana instead of trusting romaji alone.

## 3. ん: a full mora, not a fixed English “n”

| Japanese | Mora guide | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">ほん</span> | ほ・ん (2) | book |
| <span lang="ja">さんぽ</span> | さ・ん・ぽ (3) | walk, stroll |
| <span lang="ja">みんな</span> | み・ん・な (3) | everyone |
| <span lang="ja">かんじ</span> | か・ん・じ (3) | kanji; Chinese-derived characters used in Japanese |

The kana <span lang="ja">ん</span> counts as one mora. Its precise sound changes with neighboring sounds. Before <span lang="ja">ぽ</span> in <span lang="ja">さんぽ</span>, the nasal is often produced with the lips together, close to an English *m*. That **does not change the spelling** to <span lang="ja">さむぽ</span>. Before other consonants, the articulation differs. We are learning the written word and its timing here; audio listening will provide actual acoustic evidence later.

**Original example:** <span lang="ja">ほんを よみます。</span> — “I read a book / books.” The object marker <span lang="ja">を</span> is pronounced *o* in modern standard Japanese. This sentence is a contextual example, not a requirement to master the verb conjugation today.

## 4. Choose the word the situation actually needs

Read each short situation, choose the Japanese expression, and then check the explanation. These are **guided, unscored study checks**; the later interactive practice will save scored attempts.

**A.** Ren is buying a postage stamp. Is it <span lang="ja">きて</span> or <span lang="ja">きって</span>?

<details><summary>Show answer and feedback</summary><p><span lang="ja">きって</span>. The small っ is essential for “postage stamp.” <span lang="ja">きて</span> means “come!” in a familiar, casual request.</p></details>

**B.** Emi points to her grandmother's photo. Is it <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> or <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span>?

<details><summary>Show answer and feedback</summary><p><span lang="ja">おばあさん</span>. The extra あ lengthens the vowel and distinguishes “grandmother” from “aunt.” Context matters for other uses of these terms.</p></details>

**C.** Which is a three-mora word meaning “stroll”: <span lang="ja">ほん</span> or <span lang="ja">さんぽ</span>?

<details><summary>Show answer and feedback</summary><p><span lang="ja">さんぽ</span> = さ・ん・ぽ (three mora). <span lang="ja">ほん</span> = ほ・ん (two mora), meaning “book.” The ん is a full timing unit in both words.</p></details>

**D.** A sign labels an author's desk. Which is “writer”: <span lang="ja">さか</span> or <span lang="ja">さっか</span>?

<details><summary>Show answer and feedback</summary><p><span lang="ja">さっか</span> means “writer/author.” <span lang="ja">さか</span> means “slope.” Count the small っ as one mora.</p></details>

## 5. Independent retrieval and self-check

Without looking at the tables, write the hiragana for **stamp**, **grandmother**, **book**, and **writer**. Then compare with the model: <span lang="ja">きって・おばあさん・ほん・さっか</span>. Circle the extra vowel, small っ, or ん in each relevant word. If your word differs, copy the correct kana once and explain which timing unit you missed.

**Success criteria:** you can connect at least four written forms to their meanings, explain one contrast pair in a real situation, and count the mora in <span lang="ja">きって</span> and <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span>. You have **not yet demonstrated listening comprehension** or objectively measured pronunciation.
