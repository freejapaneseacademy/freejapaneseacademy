## A note for the classroom kana board

**Can-do:** Write and identify all fifteen basic vowel, K-row and S-row hiragana. Imagine a fictional classroom in which Mio needs to prepare clear labels for Sora's kana cards. First copy a character, then produce words without seeing their spelling, and finally make a short supported note.

Writing means **producing the Japanese characters**, not writing their Latin-letter readings. The scored tasks below assess exact written kana in tightly constrained prompts. Optional paper handwriting and longer messages receive **learner self-review**, never an automatic handwriting or language-proficiency grade.

## Step 1 — Observe before you write

| Vowels | K row | S row |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">あ</span> a | <span lang="ja">か</span> ka | <span lang="ja">さ</span> sa |
| <span lang="ja">い</span> i | <span lang="ja">き</span> ki | <span lang="ja">し</span> shi |
| <span lang="ja">う</span> u | <span lang="ja">く</span> ku | <span lang="ja">す</span> su |
| <span lang="ja">え</span> e | <span lang="ja">け</span> ke | <span lang="ja">せ</span> se |
| <span lang="ja">お</span> o | <span lang="ja">こ</span> ko | <span lang="ja">そ</span> so |

Read down each column. The S-row character <span lang="ja">し</span> is conventionally romanized **shi**; it is not written with the Latin letters *s-h-i*. Some Japanese IMEs accept either `shi` or `si` as input. Confirm the resulting Japanese character before submitting.

When comparing <span lang="ja">あ</span> and <span lang="ja">お</span>, notice the small separate mark on <span lang="ja">お</span>. Compare <span lang="ja">き</span> and <span lang="ja">さ</span> by their whole shapes rather than assuming the bottom curves are interchangeable. Font styles can join or separate some parts without changing the kana.

## Step 2 — Produce a character from its reading

Enable a Japanese keyboard or IME, in hiragana mode. Try typing *ka*, *shi* and *so* to produce <span lang="ja">か・し・そ</span>. On a phone you may use a Japanese flick keyboard. If your IME converts the word to kanji, select the hiragana candidate. Do not submit romaji as though it were kana.

Cover the chart. Use the fifteen scored prompts in the writing studio to produce every target character once. You may reveal model answers **after** checking. Each question has a stable identifier and a rationale. The score is a measure of exact constrained kana production, not penmanship.

## Step 3 — Build meaningful short labels

A single kana is usually a sound symbol, not a word meaning. Combining familiar symbols creates useful words:

| Word | Pieces | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">かさ</span> | か + さ | umbrella |
| <span lang="ja">すし</span> | す + し | sushi |
| <span lang="ja">いす</span> | い + す | chair |
| <span lang="ja">ここ</span> | こ + こ | here |
| <span lang="ja">せかい</span> | せ + か + い | world |

All these words use only the fifteen kana studied so far. Cover the word column and write each label from its English cue in the studio. In <span lang="ja">ここ</span>, repeat the **entire** <span lang="ja">こ</span> character. In <span lang="ja">すし</span>, the second kana is <span lang="ja">し</span>, not <span lang="ja">す</span>.

## Step 4 — Compose and revise

For the final fictional classroom task, prepare a two-item label list and a short location note using the familiar words. Models are shown only when you request them; they are examples, not the only valid expression. Use the rubric to check whether your characters are recognizable, your labels match the intended meanings and your spacing helps a reader.

**Optional paper practice:** copy <span lang="ja">あ・お・き・さ・し</span> into five boxes, then compare proportions and distinguishing marks with the chart. A printed font is a **shape reference**, not a verified stroke-order diagram. No software here grades stroke order, pen movements or handwritten accuracy. Keyboard composition is a complete accessible alternative.
