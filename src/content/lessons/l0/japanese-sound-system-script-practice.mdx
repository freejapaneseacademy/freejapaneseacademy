## 1. One vowel, two scripts

Japanese hiragana and katakana are different sets of written characters. The **five basic vowel categories** stay the same when the script changes.

| Vowel cue | Hiragana | Katakana | Notice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a | <span lang="ja">あ</span> | <span lang="ja">ア</span> | Both represent the open a vowel. |
| i | <span lang="ja">い</span> | <span lang="ja">イ</span> | The shapes differ, but the vowel is i. |
| u | <span lang="ja">う</span> | <span lang="ja">ウ</span> | Neither is an English letter; Japanese u is not identical to English “oo.” |
| e | <span lang="ja">え</span> | <span lang="ja">エ</span> | Do not mistake katakana エ for イ. |
| o | <span lang="ja">お</span> | <span lang="ja">オ</span> | Both write o; avoid adding an English-style glide in later speech practice. |

The Latin letters in the first column are **temporary reading or keyboard-input cues**. They are not kana and do not count as a Japanese-script answer. This lesson reviews the vowel pairs introduced in Chapter 01, then applies them to new syllable combinations.

**Guided recognition:** cover the katakana column. Which character goes with <span lang="ja">え</span>? Reveal the table: <span lang="ja">エ</span>. Now find <span lang="ja">お</span> and its partner <span lang="ja">オ</span>. You have identified symbols, not proven how they sound in a recording.

## 2. Build the k-row: five shapes, five vowels

An ordinary consonant-plus-vowel kana such as <span lang="ja">か</span> is normally **one mora**, even though its romaji cue *ka* uses two Latin letters. Read the k-row across in the same vowel order as the chart above:

| Hiragana | Temporary reading | Where it helps |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">か</span> | ka | The first character of <span lang="ja">かさ</span> (umbrella) and <span lang="ja">かお</span> (face). |
| <span lang="ja">き</span> | ki | k + i; compare its shape with <span lang="ja">け</span>. |
| <span lang="ja">く</span> | ku | k + u; its pointed shape is different from <span lang="ja">か</span>. |
| <span lang="ja">け</span> | ke | k + e; do not swap it with <span lang="ja">き</span>. |
| <span lang="ja">こ</span> | ko | k + o; two simple horizontal strokes in common printed forms. |

The temporary cue *ka* names the reading of <span lang="ja">か</span>; it is **not** an English translation. A word such as <span lang="ja">かさ</span> has a meaning, “umbrella.” This distinction matters when you read classroom labels.

**Practice:** point to <span lang="ja">け</span> and say which vowel follows k. Answer: e. Next point to <span lang="ja">き</span>; the vowel is i. Do not rely only on a vague “similar shape” impression.

## 3. Add three useful s-row characters

Only three s-row kana are needed for the familiar words in this chapter:

| Hiragana | Reading cue | Word and meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">さ</span> | sa | <span lang="ja">かさ</span> (*kasa*) — umbrella |
| <span lang="ja">す</span> | su | <span lang="ja">すし</span> (*sushi*) — sushi |
| <span lang="ja">し</span> | shi | <span lang="ja">すし</span> (*sushi*) — sushi |

The conventional beginner spelling *shi* reflects the Japanese sound of <span lang="ja">し</span>; a different romanization system can use *si*. **Neither spelling is the actual kana.** Also, a printed *su* does not guarantee a fully voiced u in every spoken environment. Real sound recognition needs recorded examples.

**Look and explain:** <span lang="ja">すし</span> has two characters, <span lang="ja">す</span> and <span lang="ja">し</span>, so in this basic example it has two morae. It names one food, not two separate foods. The character count and the number of words are different questions.

## 4. Keep the extra kana when a vowel is long

Writing is one way to **see** mora timing. In the examples below, the extra vowel kana changes the word or its function:

| Written form | Separate morae | Meaning in this situation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">いえ</span> | <span lang="ja">い・え</span> (2) | house (picture label) |
| <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> | <span lang="ja">い・い・え</span> (3) | no (reply) |
| <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> | <span lang="ja">お・ば・さ・ん</span> (4) | aunt (family chart) |
| <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> | <span lang="ja">お・ば・あ・さ・ん</span> (5) | grandmother (family chart) |

In <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span>, the extra <span lang="ja">あ</span> is part of the long a. The final <span lang="ja">ん</span> is also a mora. These family terms can describe people outside a family context, but the chart makes the intended meanings clear. You do not have to type the entire family-chart vocabulary today; recognize the contrast.

**Important limit:** not every long vowel is written by doubling the same kana. For instance, <span lang="ja">こうこう</span> (“high school”) conventionally uses <span lang="ja">う</span> to spell its long o vowels. The goal is to preserve the **actual spelling** of a learned word, not to invent a universal doubling rule. The later sound-change chapter will expand these patterns.

The topic particle <span lang="ja">は</span> in <span lang="ja">これは</span> is pronounced *wa* in this use. This was previewed in Chapter 01; you are not being tested on particle grammar here.

### Three mistakes to catch before they become habits

1. **Typing Latin letters instead of kana.** Entering *ka* on a Latin keyboard is only an IME input method; the final Japanese character should be <span lang="ja">か</span>.
2. **Mixing up similar forms.** <span lang="ja">き</span> (*ki*) and <span lang="ja">け</span> (*ke*) are not interchangeable; check the whole printed shape and the vowel.
3. **Dropping a timing unit.** <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> (no) contains two consecutive <span lang="ja">い</span>. <span lang="ja">いえ</span> (house) is a different word. Loudness or English stress does not replace the missing character.
