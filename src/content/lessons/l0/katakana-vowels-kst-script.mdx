## At a station kiosk

**Can-do:** Read and write katakana vowel, K, S and T rows. A kiosk displays labels in katakana. Your job is to identify each printed symbol, read a short label, and type it accurately. This lesson is **written script practice**, not a listening or pronunciation assessment.

Katakana and hiragana often represent the same sounds, but they are different writing systems. Katakana frequently appears in loanwords, names and product labels. It is not a word-for-word replacement for hiragana.

| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Vowels | <span lang="ja">ア</span> a | <span lang="ja">イ</span> i | <span lang="ja">ウ</span> u | <span lang="ja">エ</span> e | <span lang="ja">オ</span> o |
| K | <span lang="ja">カ</span> ka | <span lang="ja">キ</span> ki | <span lang="ja">ク</span> ku | <span lang="ja">ケ</span> ke | <span lang="ja">コ</span> ko |
| S | <span lang="ja">サ</span> sa | <span lang="ja">シ</span> shi | <span lang="ja">ス</span> su | <span lang="ja">セ</span> se | <span lang="ja">ソ</span> so |
| T | <span lang="ja">タ</span> ta | <span lang="ja">チ</span> chi | <span lang="ja">ツ</span> tsu | <span lang="ja">テ</span> te | <span lang="ja">ト</span> to |

**Notice:** The S row has <span lang="ja">シ</span> (*shi*), not *si* in the usual beginner romanization; the T row has <span lang="ja">チ</span> (*chi*) and <span lang="ja">ツ</span> (*tsu*). These readings are not spelling mistakes.

## Look carefully before choosing

- <span lang="ja">シ</span> (*shi*) and <span lang="ja">ツ</span> (*tsu*) are easily confused. In typical printed fonts, the short marks of シ angle more sideways and the long stroke rises from the lower left; ツ's short marks point more downward and its long stroke curves from the upper right. Compare the complete symbol, not one dot alone.
- <span lang="ja">ク</span> (*ku*) and <span lang="ja">ケ</span> (*ke*) share angled lines, but ケ has an additional stroke.
- <span lang="ja">ス</span> (*su*) and <span lang="ja">ヌ</span> (*nu*, a later row) are different. Do not add a crossing stroke when copying ス.
- <span lang="ja">ア</span> (*a*) and <span lang="ja">オ</span> (*o*) both have strong strokes, but their shapes are not interchangeable.

These shape descriptions are visual hints, not a universal handwriting-stroke-order guide. Use a reputable kana stroke-order reference before practising formal stroke order.

## Read labels in context

| Kiosk label | What you can identify |
| --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">ココア</span> | *ko-ko-a*: cocoa, a common loanword |
| <span lang="ja">カカオ</span> | *ka-ka-o*: cacao |
| <span lang="ja">アイス</span> | *a-i-su*: ice cream in a food context; also “ice” elsewhere |
| <span lang="ja">テキスト</span> | *te-ki-su-to*: text or textbook, depending on context |

Read the letters from left to right. The label <span lang="ja">アイス</span> does not mean every kind of frozen water in every context; at a food kiosk it usually refers to ice cream. A label may contain sounds introduced in later lessons; here every example uses only today's vowel, K, S and T symbols.

## Supported writing and IME practice

Set your keyboard's Japanese input to **katakana** or convert hiragana to katakana. Try typing *kokoa* → <span lang="ja">ココア</span>, *kakao* → <span lang="ja">カカオ</span>, and *ais u* without the space → <span lang="ja">アイス</span> (type **aisu**). Confirm the converted characters visually. Do not assume the first IME suggestion is always correct.

On paper, copy **ア イ ウ エ オ**, then **カ キ ク ケ コ**, **サ シ ス セ ソ**, and **タ チ ツ テ ト**. Compare each copy with the printed table. Optional tracing is ungraded: mark whether you kept the number of strokes, direction and overall proportions recognizable. If using a touch screen, a finger or stylus is fine.
