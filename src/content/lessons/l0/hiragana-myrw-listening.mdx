## Listen for meaning, not letter shapes

You have learned the remaining basic hiragana, including **ま・み・む・め・も**, **や・ゆ・よ**, **ら・り・る・れ・ろ**, and **わ・を・ん**. Hearing Japanese is different from reading it. The aim here is to understand what a speaker wants or asks someone to do. You do not need to identify a written character from a sound.

The recordings feature two fictional everyday conversations spoken by licensed synthetic AivisSpeech voices. Some familiar words may be heard in a longer phrase. Listen to the whole exchange and use the response as context.

## How to practise

Play each recording once without looking at the transcript. Listen again for one specific detail. Answer the three questions for that recording, then submit. Replay is welcome. The Japanese and English transcripts are hidden at first; revealing them before scoring makes the attempt **assisted practice**, not an unaided listening score.

### Track A — At a fruit shop

A customer asks whether a fruit is available. The shopkeeper names two fruits, and the customer requests a particular number of one fruit. Listen for the item, the available choices and the quantity. In Japanese, **ください** can politely request an item.

### Track B — In a classroom

A teacher directs a learner's attention to a book. The learner describes what the book is about, and the teacher gives a second instruction. Listen for the object, the topic and the requested action. **〜てください** is a polite way to ask someone to do something; you can understand the request without needing to master the whole grammar pattern today.

## Listening and scoring

Use the audio players and six multiple-choice questions below. All six questions ask about information stated in speech. The correct answer and the precise spoken evidence appear after submission, with explanations for review. Scores and lesson completion are saved locally in this browser; no microphone, sign-in or recording of your voice is required.

## Review with the transcript

After scoring, reveal each speaker's Japanese and English lines. In Track A, compare **もも** (peach) and **みかん** (mandarin orange), and notice **ふたつ** (two items). In Track B, compare **みてください** (please look) with **よんでください** (please read). The written particle **を** is usually pronounced *o* in these sentences; a listening question tests the meaning of the request, not how that particle is spelled.

If audio is inaccessible, you may reveal the transcript for text-supported study. That is valuable practice, but it is not evidence of unaided listening ability.
