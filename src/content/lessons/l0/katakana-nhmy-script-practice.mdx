## A shop shelf and two visitor cards

**Can-do:** Read and write the basic katakana N/H/M/Y rows. At a fictional shop, a shelf label reads <span lang="ja">ハム</span> (*ham*), while visitors wear cards marked <span lang="ja">ミナ</span> (*Mina*) and <span lang="ja">ユミ</span> (*Yumi*). Your job is to **copy what is printed**, not guess whether every katakana label names food.

This is a **written script and typing** lesson. It does not test listening, accent or handwriting quality. The later listening lesson provides genuine recorded words.

## Learn the eighteen forms

Read each row from left to right. Cover the readings and identify each character again before moving on.

| Row | Characters | Readings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| N | <span lang="ja">ナ ニ ヌ ネ ノ</span> | na, ni, nu, ne, no |
| H | <span lang="ja">ハ ヒ フ ヘ ホ</span> | ha, hi, fu, he, ho |
| M | <span lang="ja">マ ミ ム メ モ</span> | ma, mi, mu, me, mo |
| Y | <span lang="ja">ヤ ユ ヨ</span> | ya, yu, yo |

The modern **basic Y row has three symbols**, not five. Do not invent separate basic katakana for *yi* and *ye*. <span lang="ja">フ</span> is conventionally romanized *fu* in beginner charts, though its actual Japanese sound is not identical to English “foo.”

## Spot confusable shapes

- <span lang="ja">ナ</span> (*na*) has a near-vertical stroke crossed near the top; <span lang="ja">メ</span> (*me*) uses crossing diagonals. Read the **whole character**, not one isolated stroke.
- <span lang="ja">ミ</span> (*mi*) has three separate short strokes; <span lang="ja">ム</span> (*mu*) has an angular structure ending in a short mark.
- <span lang="ja">ハ</span> (*ha*) has two separated descending strokes; <span lang="ja">ヘ</span> (*he*) is joined into a peak.
- <span lang="ja">ユ</span> (*yu*) is more open than <span lang="ja">ヨ</span> (*yo*), whose horizontal levels make it look like a squared-off shape.
- <span lang="ja">マ</span> (*ma*) and <span lang="ja">ム</span> (*mu*) are not interchangeable; compare their upper and lower strokes.

Font designs vary slightly. Use legible Japanese typography and inspect the complete shape. These descriptions are recognition cues, **not certified stroke-order diagrams**.

## Guided copying with a Japanese IME

Set your keyboard to Japanese input, enter the romaji, and convert to **katakana**. On many IMEs a katakana conversion option or shortcut is available; its exact keys depend on the device. Do not assume Latin letters are already katakana.

| Model | Segment it | What it labels in our fictional scene |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">ハム</span> | ハ + ム | shelf product: ham |
| <span lang="ja">ミナ</span> | ミ + ナ | visitor name: Mina |
| <span lang="ja">ユミ</span> | ユ + ミ | visitor name: Yumi |
| <span lang="ja">ナマ</span> | ナ + マ | reading practice *na-ma*; not a product claim |

Try typing <span lang="ja">ハム</span> and <span lang="ja">ミナ</span> without looking at the models, then compare each glyph. The katakana spelling of a word does not by itself prove its role as a person's name or a product. For example, <span lang="ja">ミナ</span> is a name **in this scene** because the card is worn by a visitor.

## Optional pencil tracing and self-check

Copy the 18 forms on paper in four rows. For each symbol, compare the printed model with your attempt: **(1)** does it remain recognizable? **(2)** are separate strokes separate? **(3)** have you confused ナ/メ, ミ/ム, ハ/ヘ or ユ/ヨ? Repeat any weak form. This is self-assessment, not a machine-graded stroke-order or handwriting evaluation.

## Independent practice

Complete ten questions mixing actual katakana entry and written recognition. Type **katakana**, not hiragana, when prompted. Answers and rationales are withheld until submission; the written score measures only these questions. Attempts can be saved locally, and the lesson-completion control is separate.

**Review:** Recopy the forms you missed, and then use a new product-label reading passage in L05 to apply the same shapes. Independent qualified Japanese and assessment reviews remain pending.
