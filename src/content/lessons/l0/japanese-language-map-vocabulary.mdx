## Your first five minutes in a Japanese classroom

Imagine an instructor pointing to a word on a screen. You may not yet know the characters, but you can still recognize what the teacher is asking, request repetition, and ask whether you need a **reading** or a **meaning**.

Today you will learn a small, practical set of words rather than a whole kana chart. **Your goal is recognition and useful retrieval, not kanji memorization or perfect pronunciation.** Japanese examples use normal writing with optional furigana; readings and English explanations are supplied. Some expressions are best learned as complete polite phrases before you study their grammar.

  <span class="foundation-scenario__caption">A fictional classroom</span>
  <div><strong lang="ja"><ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby></strong><span>Teacher (address or title)</span></div>

Notice that the student can make a polite request without knowing how to conjugate verbs yet. You will meet the grammar behind these expressions later.

## 1. Four script names you can actually use

| Word | Beginner reading cue | Meaning and context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">ひらがな</span> | *hiragana* | Hiragana: rounded-looking kana, used for many words and grammar parts. |
| <span lang="ja">カタカナ</span> | *katakana* | Katakana: often used for loanwords, names and emphasis. |
| <span lang="ja"><ruby>漢字<rt>かんじ</rt></ruby></span> | *kanji* | Kanji: characters used in many Japanese words; readings depend on the word. |
| <span lang="ja">ローマ<ruby>字<rt>じ</rt></ruby></span> | *rōmaji* | Romaji: Japanese represented with Latin letters, not an English translation. |

Compare the written word <span lang="ja">さくら</span> with its Latin-letter spelling *sakura*. The **language and meaning stay the same**; the writing representation changes. The script name *romaji* does not mean "English."

A beginner-friendly way to practise rhythm is to tap the small Japanese timing units (morae): <span lang="ja">ひ・ら・が・な</span> has four; <span lang="ja">カ・タ・カ・ナ</span> has four; <span lang="ja">か・ん・じ</span> has three, because <span lang="ja">ん</span> has its own beat. In <span lang="ja">ロー・マ・じ</span>, the long-vowel mark <span lang="ja">ー</span> extends the vowel for another beat. These are **written timing guides, not recorded pronunciation models**. Your precise vowel sounds and rhythm will be developed in the dedicated sound and listening lessons.

## 2. The words behind a question

| Japanese | Reading cue | What it means |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja"><ruby>文字<rt>もじ</rt></ruby></span> | *moji* | a written character, or characters in general |
| <span lang="ja">ことば</span> (<span lang="ja"><ruby>言葉<rt>ことば</rt></ruby></span>) | *kotoba* | a word, expression or language, depending on context |
| <span lang="ja">ふりがな</span> | *furigana* | small kana supplied as a reading guide, often above kanji |
| <span lang="ja"><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby></span> | *yomikata* | how something is read; its reading |
| <span lang="ja"><ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby></span> | *imi* | meaning |

**Character versus word:** <span lang="ja">山</span> is one written character (*moji*), while <span lang="ja">やま</span> is a word (*kotoba*) meaning "mountain." One word can contain several characters; the categories are not interchangeable.

**Reading versus meaning:** <span lang="ja"><ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby></span> has the reading <span lang="ja">やま</span> (*yama*) and the meaning "mountain." A reading tells you **what to say**; a meaning tells you **what it conveys**. In the classroom, asking for one does not automatically answer the other.

**Furigana versus hiragana:** Furigana are **reading annotations** (usually written in kana), not a fifth main script. Hiragana is a script; furigana is a job small kana can perform. The small <span lang="ja">やま</span> above <span lang="ja">山</span> is furigana.

<div class="foundation-example" role="group" aria-label="Reading and meaning comparison">
  <p lang="ja"><ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby></p>
  <p><strong>Reading:</strong> やま (*yama*) · <strong>Meaning:</strong> mountain</p>
</div>

**Quick retrieval pause:** Cover the English column above. Which term would you ask for if you knew how a word sounded but not what it meant? Answer: <span lang="ja"><ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby></span>, the meaning. If you knew its meaning but not how to pronounce it, ask for <span lang="ja"><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby></span>, the reading.

## 3. Polite classroom expressions as useful chunks

You do not have to analyze every verb ending yet. Treat the following as **complete expressions** appropriate when addressing a teacher or classmate politely.

| Japanese | Reading support | Natural English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja"><ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby></span> | *sensei* | teacher; also a respectful way to address a teacher |
| <span lang="ja"><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てください。</span> | *mite kudasai* | Please look. |
| <span lang="ja"><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでください。</span> | *yonde kudasai* | Please read. |
| <span lang="ja">もういちどおねがいします。</span> | *mō ichido onegai shimasu* | One more time, please. |

The two commands are **not** the same. If the teacher says <span lang="ja"><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てください</span>, you should **look**; <span lang="ja"><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでください</span> asks you to **read**. Both use <span lang="ja">ください</span> to make a request; you will study the grammar later.

<span lang="ja">もういちどおねがいします</span> asks for repetition. It does **not** literally mean "speak more slowly." In an actual classroom you can also ask <span lang="ja">ゆっくりおねがいします</span> ("Slowly, please"), but that is a bonus expression rather than a required word today.

The long vowel in <span lang="ja">もう</span> is important. For a written rhythm reminder, notice <span lang="ja">も・う</span> takes two beats. The lesson has **no audio recording**, so you should not use these cues as proof of listening or speaking accuracy.

## 4. Two questions that solve different problems

Here is a realistic exchange. The Japanese is written normally without spaces. You may read it slowly, using the small furigana above unfamiliar kanji.

**Student:** <span lang="ja">せんせい、このことばはなんと<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みますか。</span>  
*Teacher, how do you read this word?*

**Teacher:** <span lang="ja">「さくら」と<ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>みます。</span>  
*It's read "sakura."*

**Student:** <span lang="ja">どういう<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby>ですか。</span>  
*What does it mean?*

**Teacher:** <span lang="ja">「cherry blossom」という<ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby>です。</span>  
*It means "cherry blossom."*

The teacher's English gloss is intentional: this is a beginner bilingual classroom, not a model for every Japanese conversation. You are learning to distinguish the **question about reading** from the **question about meaning**. The grammar of <span lang="ja">なんと</span> and <span lang="ja">どういう</span> is not a mastery target in this lesson.

Try replacing <span lang="ja">さくら</span> with <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span>. You already know from Lesson 01 that the latter uses katakana. Its meaning is "coffee," but the Japanese word is pronounced according to Japanese sound patterns, not exactly like the English word.

## 5. Common mix-ups to catch early

1. **Reading is not meaning.** <span lang="ja"><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>み<ruby>方<rt>かた</rt></ruby></span> asks how to say or read a written word; <span lang="ja"><ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby></span> asks what it means.
2. **Furigana is not a separate alphabet.** It is a way of displaying small kana to help with readings.
3. **Romaji is not English.** *Sakura* is a Latin-letter spelling of Japanese, not an English translation.
4. **A word is not always one character.** <span lang="ja">ことば</span> refers to a word or expression; <span lang="ja"><ruby>文字<rt>もじ</rt></ruby></span> refers to written characters.
5. **Look and read are different actions.** <span lang="ja"><ruby>見<rt>み</rt></ruby>てください</span> and <span lang="ja"><ruby>読<rt>よ</rt></ruby>んでください</span> cannot always be substituted.
6. **Polite chunks are not one-to-one word translations.** <span lang="ja">もういちどおねがいします</span> is a natural request for repetition; learn its situation before dissecting its parts.

## 6. A tiny independent study routine

Read the first two tables once. Then hide the English meanings and try to recall the script names and the difference between a **reading** and a **meaning**. Finally imagine a teacher pointing at a word: say or type the expression for "Please look" and the phrase asking for repetition. Use the flashcards and the interactive check below to verify your recall.

**Optional written rehearsal:** Copy <span lang="ja">ひらがな</span> or <span lang="ja">カタカナ</span> using a Japanese keyboard if you have one. If you do not, writing *hiragana* or *katakana* in romaji is acceptable for today's typing check. Do not grade your own handwriting from its appearance alone.

Your score is **practice accuracy**, not a certificate of pronunciation or mastery.
