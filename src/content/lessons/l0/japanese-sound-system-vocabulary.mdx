## From five sound cards to words you can recognize

In the previous lesson, Emi showed Ren the five vowel cards <span lang="ja">あ・い・う・え・お</span>. Today the cards become labels for real objects and colors. You will learn to **recognize, retrieve and explain** a small group of Japanese words rather than memorizing an entire kana chart. A word's *reading* tells you how to say it; its *meaning* tells you what it refers to; its *use* depends on the situation.

**Chapter goal:** eventually distinguish five vowel qualities and Japanese mora timing. This is **L02 word study**. Written pronunciation cues and optional aloud rehearsal help you prepare, but there is **no recording in this lesson**. The later listening step will test what you actually hear. No written score can certify pronunciation.

The spaces above are **beginner reading aids**. Standard Japanese usually has no spaces between these words. The sentences are polite and deliberately simple; sentence grammar is not today's assessment target. Notice that <span lang="ja">いえ</span> (house) and <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> (no) have different meanings and different written timing.

## 1. The five vowels: symbols, not English letter names

| Hiragana | Temporary label | Reading cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">あ</span> | *a* | A steady open vowel; not English “ay.” |
| <span lang="ja">い</span> | *i* | A steady high-front vowel; not English “eye.” |
| <span lang="ja">う</span> | *u* | Usually less lip-rounded than English “oo” in *food*. |
| <span lang="ja">え</span> | *e* | A steady mid-front vowel; avoid English “day” with its ending glide. |
| <span lang="ja">お</span> | *o* | A steady back vowel; avoid English “ow” with its ending glide. |

These are **approximate articulation guides**, not a substitute for Japanese audio. The Latin letters *a, i, u, e, o* name the five Japanese vowel categories; do not read them as English alphabet names. The Japanese <span lang="ja">う</span> varies with speaker and surrounding sounds, and its usual lip posture is not identical to the English “oo.”

**Guided check:** cover the first column. Which kana belongs to *e*? Reveal the table: <span lang="ja">え</span>. Which belongs to *o*? <span lang="ja">お</span>. This is symbol recognition, not an acoustic test.

## 2. Build a syllable with a consonant and a vowel

Japanese kana such as <span lang="ja">か</span> represent a consonant-plus-vowel unit: *k + a → ka*. A simple kana like this is normally **one mora**, not two. The same vowel can follow different consonants.

| Kana | Temporary label | Useful clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">か</span> | *ka* | First unit in <span lang="ja">かさ</span> (umbrella) and <span lang="ja">かお</span> (face). |
| <span lang="ja">き</span> | *ki* | A *k* followed by the <span lang="ja">い</span> vowel. |
| <span lang="ja">く</span> | *ku* | A *k* followed by the <span lang="ja">う</span> vowel. |
| <span lang="ja">け</span> | *ke* | A *k* followed by the <span lang="ja">え</span> vowel. |
| <span lang="ja">こ</span> | *ko* | A *k* followed by the <span lang="ja">お</span> vowel. |
| <span lang="ja">さ</span> | *sa* | Second unit in <span lang="ja">かさ</span>. |
| <span lang="ja">す</span> | *su* | First unit in <span lang="ja">すし</span>; the *u* can be weakened or devoiced in natural speech. |
| <span lang="ja">し</span> | *shi* | Second unit in <span lang="ja">すし</span>; the conventional spelling is *shi*, not *si* in the beginner romaji used here. |

These are **sample syllables**, not eight separate vocabulary words to translate. <span lang="ja">か</span> has the reading *ka*; <span lang="ja">かさ</span> is a **word** meaning umbrella. Do not confuse a reading label with an English definition.

**Try the pattern:** read the k-row in order, <span lang="ja">か・き・く・け・こ</span>. Point to the vowel in each temporary label: *a, i, u, e, o*. Each of these kana normally counts as one mora. The dedicated script lesson will develop full kana recognition and typing.

## 3. Six useful picture-card words

| Word | Reading and mora guide | Meaning in our scene |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">いえ</span> | *ie* · <span lang="ja">い・え</span> (2) | house; the building pictured on the card |
| <span lang="ja">いす</span> | *isu* · <span lang="ja">い・す</span> (2) | chair; a piece of furniture |
| <span lang="ja">かお</span> | *kao* · <span lang="ja">か・お</span> (2) | face; a person's face |
| <span lang="ja">かさ</span> | *kasa* · <span lang="ja">か・さ</span> (2) | umbrella; the object used in rain or sun |
| <span lang="ja">すし</span> | *sushi* · <span lang="ja">す・し</span> (2) | sushi; a food, not the name of an individual kana |
| <span lang="ja">あお</span> | *ao* · <span lang="ja">あ・お</span> (2) | blue; the **name of the color** |

The vocabulary words are written in hiragana here for learning. In ordinary Japanese, <span lang="ja">いえ</span> is often written <span lang="ja"><ruby>家<rt>いえ</rt></ruby></span>, <span lang="ja">かお</span> as <span lang="ja"><ruby>顔<rt>かお</rt></ruby></span>, and <span lang="ja">あお</span> as <span lang="ja"><ruby>青<rt>あお</rt></ruby></span>. **You are not expected to memorize these kanji yet.** The same spoken word can appear with different writing choices.

**Practice in context:** Emi points to an umbrella, then a chair. Which word changes? <span lang="ja">かさ</span> means umbrella; <span lang="ja">いす</span> means chair. The words share a simple two-mora pattern, but their meanings are not interchangeable.

## 4. A color word changes its job in a sentence

Compare these two related forms:

<div class="foundation-order" role="group" aria-label="Blue color noun and adjective comparison">
  <div><span>Color name (noun)</span><strong lang="ja">あお</strong></div>
  <div><span>Adjective</span><strong lang="ja">あおい</strong></div>
  <div><span>Blue umbrella</span><strong lang="ja">あおい かさ</strong></div>
</div>

<span lang="ja">あお</span> (*ao*) is the color **blue** as a noun, for example when naming a color on a palette. <span lang="ja">あおい</span> (*aoi*) is an **i-adjective** meaning blue. It can directly describe a noun: <span lang="ja">あおい かさ</span>, “a blue umbrella.” The added <span lang="ja">い</span> is not a meaningless decoration. It is part of the adjective form, and it adds a third mora: <span lang="ja">あ・お・い</span>.

Do **not** say that every Japanese adjective is made by adding <span lang="ja">い</span> to a noun. This is a specific contrast between two related forms. Some colors also have additional conventional or cultural uses; today we use the straightforward blue umbrella context.

**Guided substitution:** If the card shows a blue umbrella, choose <span lang="ja">あおい かさ</span>, not simply <span lang="ja">あお かさ</span>. If the teacher asks you to name the color by itself, <span lang="ja">あお</span> is appropriate.

## 5. One extra vowel can change the whole word

| Japanese | Written timing | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">いえ</span> | <span lang="ja">い・え</span> · 2 morae | house |
| <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> | <span lang="ja">い・い・え</span> · 3 morae | no (a response to a question) |
| <span lang="ja">あお</span> | <span lang="ja">あ・お</span> · 2 morae | blue (color name) |
| <span lang="ja">あおい</span> | <span lang="ja">あ・お・い</span> · 3 morae | blue (adjective) |

**A common mistake:** reading <span lang="ja">いえ</span> as the English word “eye” collapses two separate Japanese vowels. A second mistake is treating <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> as another spelling of “house.” In a classroom response, <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> means “no.” Its extra <span lang="ja">い</span> matters. Timing in natural speech is not perfectly equal, but the mora distinction is real.

A further mistake is assuming the written romaji *su* in <span lang="ja">すし</span> guarantees a strongly audible *u* in every conversation. Japanese high vowels can be devoiced in some environments. This lesson only teaches the **written units**; do not infer acoustic detail from English spelling.

## 6. Apply the words in a miniature classroom

Emi shows Ren four fictional picture cards. Read each response and decide what it communicates.

- <span lang="ja">これは いえ です。</span> — “This is a house.”
- <span lang="ja">これは かお です。</span> — “This is a face.”
- <span lang="ja">あおい かさ です。</span> — “It's a blue umbrella.”
- <span lang="ja">いいえ、これは いす です。</span> — “No, this is a chair.”

The simple polite frame <span lang="ja">これは … です</span> lets you focus on which **word** fits the picture. <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> is a response meaning “no,” not an object label. You do not need to produce the whole sentence independently today.

**Independent retrieval:** cover the English column in the vocabulary table. Recall the Japanese for house, chair, face, umbrella, sushi and blue. Next cover the Japanese column and retrieve the forms. Then explain why <span lang="ja">あおい かさ</span> contains three morae in <span lang="ja">あおい</span> and two in <span lang="ja">かさ</span>. The flashcards and scored check below provide feedback.

## 7. Common mix-ups and your next step

1. **Kana versus word:** <span lang="ja">か</span> reads *ka*; <span lang="ja">かさ</span> means umbrella. One sound unit is not automatically a noun.
2. **Reading versus translation:** *ie* is a reading cue for <span lang="ja">いえ</span>; “house” is its meaning in the picture-card scene.
3. **Color name versus adjective:** <span lang="ja">あお</span> names blue; <span lang="ja">あおい</span> describes a blue object.
4. **House versus no:** <span lang="ja">いえ</span> and <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> are different words with different mora counts.
5. **Romaji versus audio:** *shi* is a useful spelling cue for <span lang="ja">し</span>, not an English pronunciation guarantee.

After the written quiz, try the optional self-review prompt: explain a blue umbrella using <span lang="ja">あおい</span> and <span lang="ja">かさ</span>.
