## Hear a connected idea, not a spelling test

You can now read familiar all-hiragana words. Listening asks a different question: can you understand what a person says without seeing the writing? In these two original mini-passages, listen for **what** the speakers choose or describe and **where** an action happens. You do not need to name a kana character or guess how a word is spelled.

The voices are synthetic AivisSpeech performances of original academy conversations. Listen once for the overall situation and once for details. Replay whenever you need to; this is untimed practice.

## Track A — A classroom picture-card activity

Two people are choosing animal picture cards. One asks which card to look at; another suggests an additional card. Listen for the first animal and the second animal. The useful pattern **〜も** means “also” or “too”; you may recognize it from the conversation even if you do not know every word.

## Track B — A book at the library

A reader asks about pictures in a book. The other speaker describes the book and its illustrations. Listen for its topic, the two things in the pictures, and where the reader decides to read. **ここで** means “here, at this place.” In everyday speech, the context makes that place clear.

## Your listening check

Each recording has three original multiple-choice questions. Start without the transcript. After submitting, you can see the precise spoken phrase that supports each answer, and reveal Japanese and English lines for study. Revealing a transcript before scoring turns the attempt into **assisted practice**, not an unaided listening result.

If you cannot play the audio, reveal the transcript and study the exchange as a reading activity instead. A transcript-supported answer is useful learning but must not be presented as blind listening proficiency. Your results and lesson completion are stored locally on your device; no account or microphone is needed.

## Review the two passages

In Track A, notice the sequence: picture cards → a cat card → a dog card **also**. In Track B, listen for the book topic first, then for the illustrations and the reader's final decision. Do not confuse **what the book is about** with **what its pictures show**.
