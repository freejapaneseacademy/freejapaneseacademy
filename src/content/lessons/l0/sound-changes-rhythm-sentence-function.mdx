## A message can change in one timing unit

Imagine two students preparing picture labels for a classroom display. One card shows a **map**; another shows a **stamp**. Their teacher asks them to read each label carefully before attaching it. In Japanese, a small written change can alter a word even when the English translation seems unrelated.

**Chapter Can-do:** recognize long vowels, the small <span lang="ja">っ</span>, and the nasal mora <span lang="ja">ん</span>. This lesson focuses on **sentence function and meaning**. You will interpret written examples and count timing units. You are **not** being tested on what you can hear or how well you pronounce a word: that requires real recordings and different evidence in L06.

### Three ways a mora matters

| Feature | Written example | Timing units | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ordinary vowels | <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> | お・ば・さ・ん (4) | aunt, or a middle-aged woman, depending on context |
| Long vowel | <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> | お・ば・あ・さ・ん (5) | grandmother, or an elderly woman, depending on context |
| Small <span lang="ja">っ</span> | <span lang="ja">きって</span> | き・っ・て (3) | postage stamp |
| Nasal <span lang="ja">ん</span> | <span lang="ja">ほん</span> | ほ・ん (2) | book |

A **mora** is a Japanese timing unit. These separators are teaching marks, not normal punctuation in Japanese words. A mora is not necessarily one English syllable, and the units do not all last an identical number of milliseconds in natural conversation.

## 1. Long vowels: keep the vowel going

Compare the family-chart labels:

- <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> — *obasan*: aunt in this chart.
- <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> — *obaasan*: grandmother in this chart.

The extra <span lang="ja">あ</span> adds a timing unit to the preceding vowel. It is not a pause, a stronger accent or a new grammatical particle. In another word, <span lang="ja">おじさん</span> (uncle) contrasts with <span lang="ja">おじいさん</span> (grandfather): the added <span lang="ja">い</span> changes the meaning.

**Spelling caution:** a long vowel is not always written by repeating the same kana. In <span lang="ja">こうこう</span> (high school), <span lang="ja">こ・う・こ・う</span> is four morae; the <span lang="ja">う</span> in this familiar spelling contributes to the long *o*. Do not assume every sequence of two vowel kana has exactly the same pronunciation in every word.

**Try it:** A card says <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span>. Count the timing units without removing <span lang="ja">ん</span>: **five**. Removing the extra <span lang="ja">あ</span> changes the label, not merely its writing style.

## 2. Small っ: hold the following consonant

The small <span lang="ja">っ</span> is called *sokuon*. Before consonants such as *t* and *k*, it represents a brief consonant closure or hold before the next consonant. It counts as **one mora**; it is not pronounced as an extra full-size <span lang="ja">つ</span>.

| Word | Mora guide | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">きて</span> | き・て (2) | Come! (casual request; from 来る) |
| <span lang="ja">きって</span> | き・っ・て (3) | stamp |
| <span lang="ja">さか</span> | さ・か (2) | slope |
| <span lang="ja">さっか</span> | さ・っ・か (3) | writer / author |

The pair <span lang="ja">きて</span> and <span lang="ja">きって</span> is particularly useful because one is an **instruction** and the other is a **noun**. They cannot be swapped in a sentence just because they look similar.

Consider these short classroom utterances:

- <span lang="ja">こっちにきて。</span> — Come over here. (Casual; used between people who can speak informally.)
- <span lang="ja">きってをみせて。</span> — Show me the stamp. (Casual.)

In the second sentence, <span lang="ja">を</span> marks the stamp as the object of “show.” The small <span lang="ja">っ</span> belongs **inside the noun**; it is not a particle. The sentences are casual teaching examples, not polite requests to a stranger.

**Common mistake:** writing <span lang="ja">きつて</span> with a full-size <span lang="ja">つ</span> is not a standard spelling of “stamp.” The difference between <span lang="ja">っ</span> and <span lang="ja">つ</span> matters.

## 3. Nasal ん: one mora, different surroundings

The kana <span lang="ja">ん</span> represents a nasal mora. It counts as a timing unit even when an English speaker might group it with a neighboring syllable.

| Word | Mora guide | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">ほん</span> | ほ・ん (2) | book |
| <span lang="ja">さんぽ</span> | さ・ん・ぽ (3) | walk / stroll |
| <span lang="ja">みんな</span> | み・ん・な (3) | everyone |
| <span lang="ja">かんじ</span> | か・ん・じ (3) | kanji characters |

The **pronunciation** of <span lang="ja">ん</span> adjusts to its environment. Before a lip consonant such as *p* in <span lang="ja">さんぽ</span>, the lips can close and the nasal may sound closer to English *m*. Before *n* in <span lang="ja">みんな</span>, the nasal joins smoothly with the following sound. In <span lang="ja">ほん</span> at the end of a word, its realization differs again. These are approximate explanations of natural variation, **not three separate kana spellings**. Keep writing <span lang="ja">ん</span> in each word.

Do not write <span lang="ja">さむぽ</span> for “stroll” just because the nasal may sound *m*-like before *p*. Likewise, <span lang="ja">みんな</span> has **three morae**, not two; the nasal and following <span lang="ja">な</span> are separate timing units.

## 4. Meaning and grammar work together

Read this original caption for a classroom display:

> <span lang="ja">これはきってです。ほんのとなりにあります。</span>
>
> This is a stamp. It is next to the book.

The first sentence labels an object using <span lang="ja">これは…です</span> (“This is …”). The second uses <span lang="ja">ほんのとなり</span> (“next to the book”) and <span lang="ja">あります</span> (“there is / it is located,” for an inanimate thing). You do not need to master every grammar form today. Notice that <span lang="ja">きって</span> identifies the **stamp**, and <span lang="ja">ほん</span> identifies the **book**. Changing <span lang="ja">きって</span> to <span lang="ja">きて</span> would turn the noun into a casual command and make the label nonsensical.

Now a student writes:

> <span lang="ja">みんなでさんぽします。</span>
>
> We all take a walk / Everyone takes a walk together.

Here <span lang="ja">みんな</span> is “everyone,” <span lang="ja">で</span> marks the group acting together, and <span lang="ja">さんぽします</span> means “take a walk.” Both <span lang="ja">みんな</span> and <span lang="ja">さんぽ</span> contain a written <span lang="ja">ん</span>, but the surrounding consonants differ. The kana stays the same.

## 5. Guided check: explain the evidence

Work through these before revealing the explanations.

1. **Which written form means “stamp”?** <span lang="ja">きて</span> or <span lang="ja">きって</span>? **Answer:** <span lang="ja">きって</span>. The small <span lang="ja">っ</span> adds a consonant-hold mora and changes the word.
2. **How many morae in <span lang="ja">さんぽ</span>?** **Answer:** three: <span lang="ja">さ・ん・ぽ</span>. The nasal counts separately, even though it can sound *m*-like before *p*.
3. **Why is <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> not simply a louder <span lang="ja">おばさん</span>?** **Answer:** the additional <span lang="ja">あ</span> represents a long vowel and changes the word's meaning in the family-chart context.
4. **What is wrong with spelling “stamp” as <span lang="ja">きつて</span>?** **Answer:** full-size <span lang="ja">つ</span> is not small <span lang="ja">っ</span>. The intended word requires the small kana.
5. **Does the nasal in <span lang="ja">さんぽ</span> become a written <span lang="ja">む</span>?** **Answer:** no. Context affects pronunciation, but the spelling remains <span lang="ja">ん</span>.

### Independent application

Write two short picture labels on paper or with a Japanese keyboard: one for **stamp** and one for **book**. Then segment each into morae, including the small <span lang="ja">っ</span> and <span lang="ja">ん</span>. Compare with the model: <span lang="ja">き・っ・て</span> (three), <span lang="ja">ほ・ん</span> (two). Check whether your small kana is visibly smaller and whether you counted the nasal.

For an extra challenge, explain in English why replacing <span lang="ja">きって</span> with <span lang="ja">きて</span> in the picture caption changes the **sentence function**, not just the number of kana.

**Self-review:** Can you (a) identify all three timing features, (b) count the units, (c) give a meaning contrast, and (d) explain why <span lang="ja">ん</span> keeps its spelling across environments? If any answer is uncertain, revisit the matching table before proceeding to the script and typing practice in L04.
