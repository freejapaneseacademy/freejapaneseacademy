## At the picture-card table

In a beginner Japanese class, **Mika**, the teacher, has picture cards of a school, a watch, a cat and a bag. **Haru**, the learner, is matching the printed Japanese labels to pictures. The task is practical: a tiny change in the printed word can point to a *different object*. Mika asks Haru to slow down and check the rhythm before choosing a card.

**Chapter Can-do:** recognize **long vowels**, the **small っ** (*sokuon*, a consonant-hold mora), and **ん** (the moraic nasal). Today is **L01: context and meaning**. You will identify who is doing what, why these distinctions matter, and what the written cards show. Actual sound discrimination is taught and assessed with real recordings in a later listening lesson; text alone is not proof that you can hear these contrasts.

### A supported classroom scene

These are original instructional lines, **not recordings**. Spaces help new readers see word boundaries; normal Japanese usually has no such spaces.

| Person | Japanese | Meaning and role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mika (teacher) | <span lang="ja">カードを みてください。</span> | “Please look at the cards.” She sets the task. |
| Haru (learner) | <span lang="ja">これは ねこですか。</span> | “Is this a cat?” Haru checks a picture. |
| Mika | <span lang="ja">はい、ねこです。</span> | “Yes, it's a cat.” |
| Mika | <span lang="ja">つぎは がっこうです。</span> | “Next is the school.” She changes cards. |
| Haru | <span lang="ja">ゆっくり おねがいします。</span> | “Slowly, please.” Haru asks for support. |

You do not have to produce the whole exchange. The key is the **situation**: Mika supplies models and pictures; Haru checks meanings and asks for a slower pace. The labels represent different things, so timing and small kana are useful rather than merely decorative.

## 1. A long vowel can change the word

Compare two printed labels:

| Label | Reading and meaning | Written mora units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> | *obasan*: aunt; also an adult woman in some contexts | お・ば・さ・ん = **4** |
| <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> | *obaasan*: grandmother; also an elderly woman in some contexts | お・ば・あ・さ・ん = **5** |

The extra <span lang="ja">あ</span> extends the preceding **a** vowel. These words can have different referents; they are not interchangeable labels for the same family relationship. English “aunt” and “grandmother” are simplified teaching glosses, and both Japanese words have other context-dependent uses.

**Guided noticing:** Haru wants the “grandmother” picture. Which card should Haru select? Find the extra <span lang="ja">あ</span> in <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span>. Counting printed units gives one more mora than <span lang="ja">おばさん</span>. A written comparison helps you *notice* length; only real spoken examples can train your ear.

## 2. Small っ is a timing unit, not a full-size つ

| Label | Meaning | Segmentation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">さか</span> | slope or hill | さ・か = **2** morae |
| <span lang="ja">さっか</span> | writer or author | さ・っ・か = **3** morae |

The small <span lang="ja">っ</span> represents a hold before the following consonant in this example. It counts as one mora, although it is **not** pronounced as an independent “tsu.” Writing the large <span lang="ja">つ</span> instead changes the sequence: <span lang="ja">さつか</span> is *not* the standard spelling of <span lang="ja">さっか</span>.

**Guided noticing:** Mika shows a picture of a person writing a book. Haru should choose <span lang="ja">さっか</span>, not <span lang="ja">さか</span>. The difference is a meaningful written and rhythmic contrast, not a decorative font variation.

## 3. ん has its own mora

The character <span lang="ja">ん</span> represents a **nasal mora**. Its actual sound can change with the following sound; do not assume it always sounds like the English letter *n* in every environment.

| Label | Meaning | Written mora units |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">ほん</span> | book | ほ・ん = **2** |
| <span lang="ja">ほんや</span> | bookstore | ほ・ん・や = **3** |
| <span lang="ja">さんぽ</span> | walk; a stroll | さ・ん・ぽ = **3** |

Before a sound such as the **p** in <span lang="ja">さんぽ</span>, the nasal is often realized with lip closure, roughly [m]. That does **not** change its spelling: it remains <span lang="ja">ん</span>. The pronunciation details will be revisited with authentic playable audio; this page makes no listening or pronunciation grade.

## 4. Try the context first, then check your evidence

**Practice A — roles and purpose.** Mika has the picture cards and gives instructions. Haru asks questions and requests slower speech. Who is the teacher, and why does Haru need to pay attention to the small written differences? Answer in your own words, then compare with this model: *Mika is the teacher. Haru needs to distinguish labels because a longer vowel or an extra timing mora can change which picture a word names.*

**Practice B — pick the label.** A card shows a writer. Choose between <span lang="ja">さか</span> and <span lang="ja">さっか</span>. **Model answer:** <span lang="ja">さっか</span> (“writer”), because the small <span lang="ja">っ</span> appears before <span lang="ja">か</span>. <span lang="ja">さか</span> means “slope.”

**Practice C — count before comparing.** Count the written mora units in <span lang="ja">ほん</span> and <span lang="ja">さんぽ</span>. **Model answer:** <span lang="ja">ほ・ん</span> has two; <span lang="ja">さ・ん・ぽ</span> has three. The <span lang="ja">ん</span> is one mora in both.

**Practice D — choose the family card.** Which spelling specifically matches the usual “grandmother” gloss: <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> or <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span>? **Model answer:** <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span>. The extra <span lang="ja">あ</span> extends the vowel. Avoid treating the two words as equivalent.

### Common beginner mistakes

- **“Every written kana is a separate spoken syllable.”** Not quite: <span lang="ja">っ</span> and <span lang="ja">ん</span> each count as a mora, but do not behave like ordinary vowel-bearing syllables.
- **“Small っ is pronounced ‘tsu.’”** No. In <span lang="ja">さっか</span> it marks a consonant hold; a full-size <span lang="ja">つ</span> would be different.
- **“ん always sounds exactly like English n.”** Its articulation changes with context, including before labial consonants.
- **“If I can identify the written difference, I can hear it.”** Written recognition and blind listening are separate abilities. Later lessons will provide actual licensed recordings and evidence-based listening checks.

## Independent use: explain a card mix-up

Imagine Haru picks the “slope” card when Mika intended “writer.” Write one or two English sentences explaining the likely confusion, naming the relevant kana and the corrected word. **Self-review model:** *Haru may have overlooked the small っ. The intended word is さっか (“writer”), not さか (“slope”).*

Check yourself: did you identify the context and roles, state what the written difference means, and avoid claiming to have heard a recording? This is **unscored self-review**. No voice or pronunciation assessment is made.
