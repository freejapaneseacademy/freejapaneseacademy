## Can-do: greet a teacher and follow a simple classroom request

Imagine arriving at a beginner Japanese class. Your teacher welcomes you; later a classmate lends you a pencil. You need to choose **the expression that fits the situation**, not just translate individual English words. This lesson practices written meaning and social context. The chapter's L06 will provide genuine listening.

## 1. A greeting depends on the time and relationship

| Expression | Reading cue | Typical use |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">おはよう</span> | ohayō | “Morning!” to a familiar person |
| <span lang="ja">おはようございます</span> | ohayō gozaimasu | polite “Good morning,” suitable for a teacher |
| <span lang="ja">こんにちは</span> | konnichiwa | “Hello / good afternoon” in daytime encounters |
| <span lang="ja">こんばんは</span> | konbanwa | “Good evening” as a greeting |

**おはようございます** is a polite morning greeting. **おはよう** is a familiar, shorter version; neither is inherently rude, but relationship and setting matter. **こんにちは** is a common daytime greeting, not a universal replacement for every morning or evening greeting. In **こんにちは**, the last kana is **は**, conventionally pronounced *wa*; do not write こんにちわ as the standard greeting.

Try the scene: at 8:30 a.m., you meet a teacher at the classroom door. Say **おはようございます**. Later, you meet a classmate you know well; **おはよう** may fit if the relationship is informal.

## 2. Thanking someone: ordinary and more polite

| Expression | Reading cue | Meaning and context |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">ありがとう</span> | arigatō | “Thanks,” often familiar or informal |
| <span lang="ja">ありがとうございます</span> | arigatō gozaimasu | polite “Thank you” |
| <span lang="ja">どうもありがとうございます</span> | dōmo arigatō gozaimasu | “Thank you very much” |

A teacher hands you a worksheet: **ありがとうございます** is a safe polite response. A close friend hands you an eraser: **ありがとう** is natural. **ございます** adds politeness here; it does **not** mean “please.” These expressions can be used with many people, and politeness depends on the social situation rather than a rigid rule about age.

## 3. Polite classroom requests: 〜てください

A teacher may say:

- <span lang="ja">みてください。</span> — “Please look.”
- <span lang="ja">きいてください。</span> — “Please listen.”
- <span lang="ja">よんでください。</span> — “Please read.”
- <span lang="ja">かいてください。</span> — “Please write.”

The **〜てください** pattern is a common polite request, formed from a verb's *te-form* plus **ください**. For now, learn these four complete classroom phrases as chunks; later lessons explain te-form conjugation. **ください** by itself can be used in some requests for things, but **みてください** is not interchangeable with **みます** (“look / will look” in polite statement form). These are requests, not descriptions of what someone is already doing.

If the teacher says **よんでください**, look at the written text and read it. If the teacher says **きいてください**, listen. In a real classroom, the teacher's gesture and activity provide additional context.

## 4. An original mini classroom scene

> **せんせい:** おはようございます。  
> **がくせい:** おはようございます。  
> **せんせい:** ここを みてください。  
> **がくせい:** はい。  
> **せんせい:** つぎを よんでください。  
> **がくせい:** はい。ありがとうございます。

**English:** Teacher: “Good morning.” Student: “Good morning.” Teacher: “Please look here.” Student: “Yes.” Teacher: “Please read the next part.” Student: “Yes. Thank you.”

The dialogue is original, simplified classroom Japanese. **ここを** means “here” as the object of looking in this phrase; **つぎを** means “the next part” in context. The student uses **はい** to acknowledge the instruction, not to claim that the reading has already been completed.

## 5. Guided comparison and production

For each situation, first identify **time**, **relationship**, and **action**:

1. Morning, speaking to a teacher → **おはようございます**.
2. Daytime, meeting a new classmate → **こんにちは**.
3. Thanking a teacher for help → **ありがとうございます**.
4. Asking learners to read → **よんでください**.

Type **こんにちは** and **ありがとうございます** using Japanese IME. Check the final **は** in こんにちは and the long **う** in ありがとう. Read the four classroom requests aloud *if comfortable*, but this lesson does not evaluate pronunciation or require a microphone.

**Common misconception:** A longer expression is not always required. Polite forms are useful with teachers and strangers, while shorter forms can be natural among familiar peers. Do not treat every casual greeting as impolite.
