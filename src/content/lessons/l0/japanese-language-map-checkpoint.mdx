## Your first chapter checkpoint

Imagine that you are helping a new learner understand a classroom poster. The poster includes <span lang="ja">あ</span>, <span lang="ja">ア</span>, <span lang="ja"><ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby></span> and *sakura*. You will also hear a man and a woman having two brief everyday conversations. Your task is to identify the scripts, find evidence on the poster, listen for what two speakers ask, respond to and decide and explain what you have learned.

**Chapter Can-do:** explain how hiragana, katakana, kanji and romaji differ. This checkpoint also revisits the preview of topic and object particles and Japanese's common verb-final pattern. It does **not** assess complete kana mastery or kanji memorization.

This is a mixed review of Lessons 01–08, not a new set of vocabulary to memorize. The 16 questions below are new questions based on the existing chapter's original reading chart and two original two-speaker WAV recordings. The reading and audio materials are intentionally reused; their answer questions are not copies of the earlier practice bank.

## 1. Prepare your evidence, not just a guess

Start by looking at the four cards. Hiragana and katakana are both *kana*: their characters generally represent Japanese sound units. They use different shapes and have different conventional roles. Kanji are characters used in many written words; they can have more than one reading depending on the word. Romaji writes Japanese with Latin letters. These are useful tendencies, **not** rules that every word follows without exception.

For example, <span lang="ja">さくら</span> is hiragana, while *sakura* is its Japanese reading written in romaji. Neither spelling is the English translation; that is *cherry blossom*. Similarly, <span lang="ja"><ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby></span> uses kanji, with <span lang="ja">やま</span> as a reading and *mountain* as its English meaning. At Level 0, you are identifying these roles, not being tested on a list of kanji readings.

## 2. Review how a short Japanese sentence is organized

A common beginning pattern is **topic + は / object + を / action verb**. Compare the following examples:

<div class="foundation-example">
  <p lang="ja">わたしは おちゃを のみます。</p>
  <p>“I drink tea.” The particle は follows the topic わたし and is pronounced *wa* here; を follows the thing drunk, おちゃ. The polite action verb のみます appears at the end.</p>
</div>

<div class="foundation-example">
  <p lang="ja">せんせいは ひらがなを かきます。</p>
  <p>“The teacher writes hiragana.” The topic is the teacher, the thing written is hiragana, and the action is かきます (“writes”). This is a supported model, not a claim that every Japanese sentence has exactly this word order.</p>
</div>

<div class="foundation-example">
  <p lang="ja">「あ」は ひらがなです。</p>
  <p>“The character あ is hiragana.” This identifies a noun using です; it does not describe an action. Do not add です after a polite verb such as かきます.</p>
</div>

**Common mistakes to watch for:** calling <span lang="ja">ア</span> hiragana because it sounds like <span lang="ja">あ</span>; calling *sakura* an English translation; treating furigana as a separate writing system; confusing the reading <span lang="ja">やま</span> with the meaning *mountain*; pronouncing topic は as *ha*; and assuming です must follow every polite verb.

## 3. Reading evidence and listening evidence are different skills

The **reading chart** below is open for reference. Locate the row and read its example and note before selecting an answer. You should be able to point to the printed evidence. The chart is horizontally scrollable on small screens, with keyboard access.

The **two listening recordings** are original Japanese conversations with a man and a woman taking turns. Unlike the reading questions, they test spoken meaning, not the shape or spelling of written characters. For a blind listening attempt, play both before answering and keep their Japanese transcripts hidden until after submission. You may pause, replay and change speed. Revealing a transcript early changes the attempt to **assisted review**; it can still show feedback but will not be saved as a blind listening checkpoint score.

A recording is not a human pronunciation certificate. These conversations use the licensed Aida Shigeru (Calm, male) and morioki (Normal, female) voices from AivisSpeech. Their reuse does not imply a new audio asset or independent pronunciation sign-off.

## 4. How the checkpoint is scored

There are **16 single-choice questions**: eight on script roles, classroom words and sentence function; four requiring evidence from the nine-row reading chart; and four requiring details from the two audio tracks. Each item has one keyed answer and a rationale for all alternatives. You may retry after reviewing the feedback. A saved attempt is a browser-local **practice score**, not an official JLPT score, a certificate or a measure of overall fluency.

Do not rush through a guessed answer. For reading, return to the row. For listening, replay the relevant recording. For sentence function, identify the topic, the thing acted upon and the action separately. If your score is below 80%, revisit the relevant lesson and try again later; completion remains your choice regardless of score.

## 5. Independent writing and optional speaking

After the scored questions, write a short explanation for an imaginary classmate: distinguish all four writing systems, include one example of kana versus romaji and explain the difference between a Japanese reading and an English meaning. A model answer and a criteria-based self-review checklist can be revealed when you are ready. **Your prose is not automatically graded or uploaded**. Copy it yourself if you wish to keep it.

If you want speaking practice, revisit [Lesson 08](/learn/start/module-01/chapter-01/lesson-08/) and rehearse the two original recordings. Recording your voice is optional; the chapter checkpoint never requires microphone permission.
