## Your first real listening lesson

Imagine sitting in a beginner Japanese classroom. The teacher is describing the writing systems you already saw on the page. There are no long conversations or new grammar explanations: your challenge is to **recognize a familiar word when you hear it** and pick out just one or two facts. Reading and listening are different skills. You can easily recognize <span lang="ja">ひらがな</span> on a page and still need time to notice the same word in speech.

This lesson uses **two real, independent Japanese audio recordings**, not silent placeholders and not merely text disguised as a listening exercise. Both recordings were synthesized from original academy-authored teaching scripts. The Japanese audio is in a beginner-friendly narrated style; it has **not** received independent native-speaker pronunciation sign-off. A transcript is available when needed, but revealing it before the quiz changes your attempt to *assisted practice*, not a blind listening score.

### How to listen without knowing every word

In an unfamiliar language, trying to translate every sound can make you miss the important detail. Instead, listen in layers:

1. **First pass:** what kind of situation is this? You should hear a teacher greeting a class and explaining Japanese writing.
2. **Second pass:** pick out the words from earlier lessons, especially the two kana names, kanji and romaji. You do **not** need to reproduce the full sentence.
3. **Third pass:** listen for one clearly stated detail: which script is introduced first, what a character is called, or how a symbol is read.
4. **Only afterward:** compare with the written transcript, correct misunderstandings and listen again.

You may replay either recording freely. A slower playback setting can help you segment the words, but try normal speed first and return to it after slowing down.

## 1. What to listen for in Track A

**Scenario:** a teacher starts a short classroom overview and introduces the two kana scripts.

The key skill is distinguishing **writing from sound**. Two Japanese symbols can look different yet represent the same underlying sound. You already know that hiragana and katakana are not separate spoken languages. The audio now gives you a chance to *hear* the teacher make that distinction.

Pay attention to the teacher's **order words**: a phrase meaning “first” and a phrase meaning “next.” You do not need to spell them from memory. Their job is to signal sequence, so the correct answer may depend on *which word comes after the cue*.

**Guided first attempt:** play Track A once without looking at a transcript. Think: “Did I hear two writing systems, and did the narrator compare their sounds?” Then listen a second time and attempt questions 1–4.

## 2. What to listen for in Track B

**Scenario:** the same lesson continues with a kanji example, a simple hiragana word and a description of romaji.

This recording has several short, directly stated facts. A **reading** tells you how to say a written item; an **English meaning** tells you what it means. Listen for the teacher identifying a familiar kanji and giving its Japanese reading. Do not confuse that with an English translation.

The final sentence describes a method of representing Japanese using another alphabet. Listen for the familiar category name. You only need to identify the *relationship* between Japanese writing and Latin letters, not translate every syllable.

**Guided second attempt:** play Track B without opening the transcript. Pause after hearing the word for kanji, then replay and concentrate on the short phrase that follows the written-character example. Attempt questions 5–8.

## 3. Your listening workspace

## 4. Four listening mistakes beginners can correct

- **Looking for the exact English spelling in Japanese audio.** The teacher is speaking Japanese, not reading your English explanation. Focus on the familiar Japanese script names and sounds.
- **Treating a reading as a translation.** A word like *yama* is how Japanese is pronounced; “mountain” is the English meaning. The narrated example provides its reading, not an English dictionary definition.
- **Assuming two scripts must have two different vowel sounds.** A hiragana character and its katakana counterpart may represent the same Japanese vowel.
- **Thinking an unfamiliar word means the entire recording is impossible.** In this lesson every scored question asks for a **directly spoken fact**. Catching one key word plus its neighbor is enough.

## 5. After you check your answers

Open the transcripts and do a useful comparison. Take one answer you got wrong, locate the corresponding phrase in the written Japanese and replay just that portion. Can you now hear where one phrase ends and the next begins? Repeat the relevant short phrase aloud if comfortable, without treating optional speaking as a scored assignment.

Do another listening pass at normal speed without looking at the written words. If you revealed the transcript before the quiz, you can choose **Start a new blind attempt** to try without help; only a genuinely unseen attempt is saved as a blind-listening score.

### What this lesson does and does not measure

You are practising **recognition of immediately stated information** in very short original narrated overviews. Eight multiple-choice questions are not evidence of fluent listening, natural pronunciation or overall Japanese mastery. Your lesson completion is separate from the quiz score, and browser-local progress is not synchronized with an account.
