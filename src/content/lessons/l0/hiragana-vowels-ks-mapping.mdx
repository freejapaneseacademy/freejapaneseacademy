## Mio decodes the kana cards

**Chapter Can-do:** Read, write and identify <span lang="ja">あ・い・う・え・お、か・き・く・け・こ、さ・し・す・せ・そ</span>. Mio sees a new word on Sora's classroom card. Rather than guessing its English meaning, she first identifies each kana's sound. This lesson is about **how characters represent sounds**, not sentence grammar or an automatic handwriting grade.

## Fifteen character-to-sound mappings

| Vowels | K row | S row |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">あ</span> a | <span lang="ja">か</span> ka | <span lang="ja">さ</span> sa |
| <span lang="ja">い</span> i | <span lang="ja">き</span> ki | <span lang="ja">し</span> shi |
| <span lang="ja">う</span> u | <span lang="ja">く</span> ku | <span lang="ja">す</span> su |
| <span lang="ja">え</span> e | <span lang="ja">け</span> ke | <span lang="ja">せ</span> se |
| <span lang="ja">お</span> o | <span lang="ja">こ</span> ko | <span lang="ja">そ</span> so |

Each kana generally represents one **mora**, a rhythmic sound unit. For these fifteen beginner symbols, each card maps to one mora. Do not split <span lang="ja">し</span> into two Japanese morae merely because *shi* has three Latin letters. English-oriented Hepburn romanization writes **shi**, not *si*; the Japanese sound is not identical to English “she.” A more systematic romanization may write *si* for the same kana. Neither spelling changes the Japanese character.

## Read a word by combining the cards

- <span lang="ja">かさ</span> = か (*ka*) + さ (*sa*) → *kasa*, umbrella.
- <span lang="ja">すし</span> = す (*su*) + し (*shi*) → *sushi*, sushi.
- <span lang="ja">いす</span> = い (*i*) + す (*su*) → *isu*, chair.
- <span lang="ja">せかい</span> = せ (*se*) + か (*ka*) + い (*i*) → *sekai*, world.
- <span lang="ja">ここ</span> = こ (*ko*) + こ (*ko*) → *koko*, here.

**Misuse to avoid:** Reading <span lang="ja">し</span> as English “see” because it sits in the S row. The S row is not perfectly regular in common romanization. Also, combining sound labels does not reveal a word's meaning: learn *kasa* and “umbrella” as a whole vocabulary item.

## Basic stroke conventions

Japanese hiragana is written with a conventional **stroke sequence and direction**. A stroke is a continuous movement before lifting the pen. Different characters have different numbers of strokes, so do not assume that every kana in a row shares a stroke count. When you later practise tracing, follow a trusted stroke-order model for **each individual character**, begin at the indicated starting point, and keep the character within a consistent writing box. The printed kana grid above teaches recognition and sound; it is **not** an animated stroke-order diagram, and you should not infer stroke direction from its static shapes.

For now, use these safe writing habits: keep each kana visually distinct, leave a small space between practice boxes, and compare the finished form against the printed model. Lesson 04 supplies dedicated kana form, typing and optional tracing practice. Neither drawing a recognizable shape nor choosing a romanized answer proves correct stroke order.

## Guided practice

Read <span lang="ja">か・き・く・け・こ</span> in order as *ka, ki, ku, ke, ko*. Next read <span lang="ja">さ・し・す・せ・そ</span> as *sa, shi, su, se, so*. Point to <span lang="ja">し</span> and explain why it is not written *shi* in Japanese script: *shi* is only a Latin reading aid.

Mio sees <span lang="ja">せかい</span>. She segments it into <span lang="ja">せ・か・い</span>, reads *se-ka-i*, and then recalls the lexical meaning “world” from Lesson 02. She does **not** treat the kana as three English words.
