## From understanding Japanese to explaining it aloud

Imagine a beginner classroom with four cards: <span lang="ja">あ</span>, <span lang="ja">ア</span>, <span lang="ja"><ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby></span> and the Latin spelling *sakura*. A classmate asks why the cards look different. You already know the answer from the first seven lessons. Here you will **rehearse saying it**, using short phrases that a teacher actually says in two original narrated recordings.

**Today's Can-do:** pronounce the featured sounds and rehearse a short explanation of how hiragana, katakana, kanji and romaji differ. Speaking is an **optional extra skill** in this course, not an official JLPT speaking test and never a condition for completing the chapter. You may practise aloud without a microphone.

The model recordings are the same genuine WAV files introduced in Lesson 06. They were produced from original academy scripts using the Japanese MeloTTS model and are served by the academy's read-only Cloudflare audio Worker. The clips are not newly generated, and independent human pronunciation review is still pending. The phrases shown in the interactive workshop occur in those recordings; a longer teach-back assembled from them is a **practice script**, not a third recording.

## 1. What shadowing means

*Shadowing* is listening to a phrase and trying to reproduce its sounds, pauses and rhythm. At this early level, start with **listen → pause → repeat**, rather than trying to speak simultaneously with a full recording.

1. Listen to one full track at normal speed, without speaking.
2. Find the first short phrase in the printed cue list. Play the recording, pause after the phrase and repeat once.
3. Listen again at a slower speed if needed. Do not assume slowing playback gives a perfectly natural pronunciation model.
4. Repeat the same phrase while looking away from the Japanese text, then return to normal speed.
5. Explain in English what you just said. If the meaning is unclear, reread the translation before trying again.

There is no required recording count, fluency threshold or score. The goal is to practise meaningful, intelligible speech with the resources available.

## 2. Start with a classroom greeting and two kana scripts

Track A begins with <span lang="ja">みなさん、こんにちは。</span> — “Hello, everyone.” This is a friendly greeting to a group. Then the teacher moves from one script to another:

<div class="foundation-example">
  <p lang="ja">はじめは、ひらがなです。</p>
  <p>“First is hiragana.” The phrase はじめは introduces the first item. The topic particle は is written *ha* but pronounced *wa* here.</p>
</div>

<div class="foundation-example">
  <p lang="ja">つぎは、カタカナです。</p>
  <p>“Next is katakana.” The word つぎ means “next”; again, the topic particle は is pronounced *wa*. Use this phrase when changing cards.</p>
</div>

The model also names <span lang="ja">「あ」</span> as hiragana and <span lang="ja">「ア」</span> as katakana. Both represent the Japanese vowel *a*. They are **different written forms, not two separate vowel sounds**. Avoid saying the English letter name “ay” for either character. Aim for a clear, steady vowel; do not overstate that this brief exercise teaches the entire Japanese sound system.

### Guided practice: listen, point and repeat

Point to the hiragana card while saying <span lang="ja">「あ」は、ひらがなです。</span>. Then point to the katakana card and say <span lang="ja">「ア」は、カタカナです。</span>. Listen to Track A between attempts. The subject of your sentence is the character being pointed to, not the sound alone.

A common mistake is to call both cards “hiragana” because they have the same sound. Another is to pronounce the topic particle は as *ha* in these sentences. Compare the spoken model and the written labels: the **script name** changes, while the **a sound** stays the same.

## 3. Add kanji and romaji without confusing reading and meaning

Track B introduces <span lang="ja">かんじ</span> (kanji), then a familiar character:

<div class="foundation-example">
  <p lang="ja">「<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>」は、かんじです。「やま」と よみます。</p>
  <p>“The character 山 is kanji. It is read yama.” The Japanese reading is やま; the English meaning is “mountain.” The reading and the meaning are different kinds of information.</p>
</div>

You do not need to memorise kanji readings in this Level 0 lesson. The speaking task is simply to repeat the model phrase, identify the writing system and avoid calling *yama* the English translation.

The teacher then explains romaji:

<div class="foundation-example">
  <p lang="ja">ローマじは、アルファベットで にほんごを かく ほうほうです。</p>
  <p>“Romaji is a way to write Japanese using alphabet letters.” In the recording, ローマじ is a beginner-friendly mixed spelling of the standard word ローマ字. Romaji is a representation of Japanese in Latin letters, not an additional spoken language.</p>
</div>

This sentence is longer. Practise it in three meaningful chunks: <span lang="ja">ローマじは</span> / <span lang="ja">アルファベットで</span> / <span lang="ja">にほんごを かく ほうほうです</span>. The chunks are rehearsal aids, not claims about exact audio timestamps or syllable timing.

## 4. Turn four separate facts into a useful explanation

In the workshop, you will see two complete native audio players and a cue list for each. Rehearse the lines in order, then try the guided four-card teach-back. You can show a card as you speak; that makes the meaning clear even if your speech is still slow.

If someone asks “How are these different?”, you can say the recorded short labels in Japanese and add a simple English explanation: **hiragana** writes many native words and grammatical endings; **katakana** often writes loanwords and other specially marked terms; **kanji** are characters used for many word meanings; **romaji** writes Japanese with Latin letters. These are common roles, not absolute rules. Japanese sentences often combine multiple scripts.

Notice the distinction between a **label** and a **complete explanation**. <span lang="ja">「あ」は、ひらがなです。</span> labels one example. Your longer teach-back connects four examples so a listener understands their roles. It is okay to use English for the detailed explanation at Level 0.

## 5. Common speaking and listening pitfalls

- **Repeating text without checking meaning:** after each model phrase, identify the card or writing system it refers to.
- **Pronouncing は as ha in a topic phrase:** in <span lang="ja">つぎは</span> it is the topic particle pronounced *wa*. The same character can have other readings in other words.
- **Replacing Japanese vowels with English letter names:** <span lang="ja">あ</span> and <span lang="ja">ア</span> are both the vowel *a*, not the English name of the letter A.
- **Calling やま an English word:** it is the Japanese reading of 山; “mountain” is its English meaning.
- **Rushing the romaji explanation:** pause at phrase boundaries; clarity matters more than speed.
- **Assuming synthetic audio proves your pronunciation is correct:** listen critically and use the rubric. Neither this page nor a browser recording can certify spoken proficiency.
