## A classroom chart with two ways to write each vowel

Your teacher has written <span lang="ja">あ</span> on one side of a board and <span lang="ja">ア</span> on the other. These shapes look different, but they represent the **same Japanese vowel**. One belongs to **hiragana**, the other to **katakana**. This lesson moves from recognizing writing systems in whole words to noticing and producing individual written forms.

**Today's Can-do:** explain how hiragana, katakana, kanji and romaji differ by pointing to actual written examples. You will learn only **five vowel pairs**, not all kana, and you are **not** being asked to memorize kanji readings. This is the L04 *script and kanji* slot of the Japanese language map; Level 0 deliberately assigns no new kanji mastery targets.

## 1. One sound, two kana shapes

Japanese has five basic vowel qualities, often represented by the romaji letters *a, i, u, e, o*. Each vowel has a hiragana form and a katakana form. Read across each row:

| Romaji cue | Hiragana | Katakana | What to notice |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a | <span lang="ja">あ</span> | <span lang="ja">ア</span> | Both represent a, but their strokes and overall silhouettes differ. |
| i | <span lang="ja">い</span> | <span lang="ja">イ</span> | Hiragana い is curving; katakana イ is angular. |
| u | <span lang="ja">う</span> | <span lang="ja">ウ</span> | う and ウ share a vowel, not a shape. |
| e | <span lang="ja">え</span> | <span lang="ja">エ</span> | エ is angular; do not confuse it with イ. |
| o | <span lang="ja">お</span> | <span lang="ja">オ</span> | お and オ represent the same vowel in different scripts. |

The romaji letters are **reading aids**, not a third set of Japanese kana. Writing the Latin letter *a* is not the same as entering the hiragana character <span lang="ja">あ</span>. Kana are written characters, not English translations.

**Try it now:** cover the katakana column. Point to <span lang="ja">い</span>, say its romaji cue *i*, and predict its katakana partner. Reveal the answer in the interactive pair cards below. Repeat with <span lang="ja">う</span> and <span lang="ja">お</span>.

## 2. Shape recognition, not visual guessing

Compare <span lang="ja">い</span> with <span lang="ja">イ</span> and <span lang="ja">え</span> with <span lang="ja">エ</span>. The katakana shapes are often more angular, but **angular versus round is only a useful tendency**, not a foolproof script test. Learn the actual characters, not just a style rule.

A practical recognition sequence is:

1. Notice whether the printed character is <span lang="ja">あ</span> or <span lang="ja">ア</span>.
2. Name its script: hiragana or katakana.
3. Recall the shared vowel cue: *a*.
4. Reproduce the form by typing or copying it. Check the printed model; do not claim perfect stroke order from visual similarity alone.

**Common mistake:** treating <span lang="ja">ア</span> as a different vowel from <span lang="ja">あ</span>. The script changes; the vowel represented by the character does not. Another mistake is reading <span lang="ja">エ</span> as *i* because it resembles <span lang="ja">イ</span>. The vowel for <span lang="ja">エ</span> is *e*.

## 3. Connect the shapes to classroom words

Look at two words already encountered in your earlier lessons:

<div class="foundation-example" role="group" aria-label="Hiragana word example">
  <p lang="ja">さくら</p>
  <p>Hiragana さ・く・ら — *sakura*, cherry blossom.</p>
</div>

All three characters here are hiragana. The same word may appear in other scripts in different contexts, so a word's **meaning does not dictate one script in every situation**. For this classroom example, you are recognizing the written form shown, not memorizing every possible spelling.

<div class="foundation-example" role="group" aria-label="Katakana loanword example">
  <p lang="ja">コーヒー</p>
  <p>Katakana コ・ー・ヒ・ー — *kōhī*, coffee.</p>
</div>

Here the long horizontal mark <span lang="ja">ー</span> **extends the preceding vowel**. It is not the vowel <span lang="ja">イ</span>, not an English hyphen, and not a kanji. In standard katakana spelling, this mark is commonly used for long vowels, as in <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span>.

**Read the evidence:** when asked “Which word contains a katakana length mark?”, point to <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> and specifically to <span lang="ja">ー</span>. When asked “Which word is written in hiragana?”, point to <span lang="ja">さくら</span>. You will practise evidence-based reading more fully in L05.

## 4. What about kanji and furigana?

In the example <span lang="ja"><ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby></span>, the large character <span lang="ja">山</span> is **kanji**, and the small <span lang="ja">やま</span> above it is **furigana** showing its reading in this context. Its English meaning here is *mountain*. Furigana are usually small kana annotations, not a separate writing system.

You can recognize **the difference between the kanji and the reading guide** without memorizing the character's other readings, stroke order or compounds. That is all this Level 0 chapter asks. In later courses you will learn kanji within useful words.

Contrast four things:

| Example | What it is | What it is not |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">あ</span> | Hiragana vowel character | Latin-letter romaji |
| <span lang="ja">ア</span> | Katakana vowel character | A different vowel from あ |
| <span lang="ja">山</span> | Kanji character | One of today's five vowel kana |
| *yama* | Romaji spelling of a Japanese reading | English translation “mountain” |

## 5. How to type kana without pretending romaji is kana

If your device has a Japanese input method (IME), switch to **Japanese hiragana input** and try typing the Latin key *a*: many common romaji-input IMEs convert it to <span lang="ja">あ</span>. Switching to katakana conversion or choosing katakana output can produce <span lang="ja">ア</span>. The exact keys and conversion steps depend on your operating system and IME; a Latin *a* left unconverted is **not** kana.

Try this small routine with your keyboard:

1. Enter <span lang="ja">あ</span> in hiragana mode and compare it with the model above.
2. Enter <span lang="ja">い</span> and <span lang="ja">う</span>. Verify each character before moving on.
3. Switch to katakana output and enter <span lang="ja">ア</span>, <span lang="ja">イ</span> or <span lang="ja">オ</span>.
4. If Japanese input is unavailable, use the **full on-screen vowel palette** in the interactive check. This is a recognition-and-entry alternative; it does not certify IME proficiency.

The scored questions distinguish **hiragana** from **katakana**. They do not accept Latin letters as substitutes for kana, because producing the actual written form is the goal.

## 6. Optional tracing and a useful self-check

Look closely at <span lang="ja">あ</span> and <span lang="ja">ア</span>. Copy one on paper or in the optional freehand drawing area. Then compare the overall shape with the model. Do not infer correct **stroke order**, stroke direction, balance or motor skill from this freehand practice. Proper stroke-order diagrams require separately checked and licensed references; none are presented here.

**A four-part self-check:** Can you identify the script? Name the vowel? Enter the character? Explain where a similar character occurs in a word? For example, a learner who sees <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> should be able to say “katakana,” identify the length mark, and explain that the word means coffee.

## 7. Common mistakes and how to correct them

- **“Romaji is English.”** No. *Sakura* is Japanese represented with Latin letters; “cherry blossom” is an English meaning.
- **“The vowels change between scripts.”** No. <span lang="ja">あ</span> and <span lang="ja">ア</span> represent the same Japanese vowel, though their written forms differ.
- **“The mark ー is an extra vowel letter.”** No. It is a vowel-length mark, often used in katakana.
- **“Furigana are another alphabet.”** No. They are kana reading annotations, often placed above kanji.
- **“I must know all kanji before reading Japanese.”** No. At this stage, recognizing a kanji and using its furigana is sufficient.
- **“A drawing that looks similar proves correct handwriting.”** No. This lesson provides visual copying only, not validated stroke-order assessment.
