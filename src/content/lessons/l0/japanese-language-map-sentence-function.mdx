## From a word list to a meaningful sentence

You already recognize four ways Japanese can be written, and you have met words such as <span lang="ja">ひらがな</span>, <span lang="ja">カタカナ</span>, <span lang="ja"><ruby>意味<rt>いみ</rt></ruby></span> (meaning) and <span lang="ja"><ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby></span> (teacher). Now imagine your teacher writes a whole sentence:

<div class="foundation-example" role="group" aria-label="A simple Japanese sentence">
  <p lang="ja"><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はコーヒーを<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みます。</p>
  <p>I drink coffee. (Polite, neutral statement.)</p>
</div>

It is **one Japanese sentence**, even though it contains kanji, hiragana and katakana. You do **not** have to memorize the kanji today. Use the small readings (*furigana*) to follow along.

By the end of this lesson you will be able to **explain what each writing system contributes**, find two small grammatical markers, and recognize the typical **verb-final** pattern. This is a preview of sentence function, not a complete course in particles or conjugation.

## 1. The three scripts have different jobs, not separate languages

Look at the same sentence in parts:

| Part | Reading / meaning | What the writing does |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja"><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby></span> | *watashi* — I | Kanji writes this word; its reading is shown above. |
| <span lang="ja">は</span> | pronounced *wa* here | Hiragana writes the **topic particle**. |
| <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> | *kōhī* — coffee | Katakana writes a common loanword. |
| <span lang="ja">を</span> | pronounced *o* here | Hiragana marks the **direct object**. |
| <span lang="ja"><ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みます</span> | *nomimasu* — drink | Kanji writes the verb stem; hiragana completes the word with an ending. |

**Important:** a *particle* is a short grammatical element that helps show a word's role. The script does not determine the whole grammatical role by itself: hiragana can write ordinary words as well as particles, and kanji can appear in many kinds of words. The ending <span lang="ja">みます</span> is part of the verb, not a separate English-style word.

You can also write a simple sentence **entirely in hiragana**:

<div class="foundation-example" role="group" aria-label="All-hiragana example">
  <p lang="ja">わたしはおちゃをのみます。</p>
  <p>I drink tea. The absence of kanji does not make this a different language or sentence order.</p>
</div>

The usual mixed-script spelling is <span lang="ja"><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はお<ruby>茶<rt>ちゃ</rt></ruby>を<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みます。</span> Kanji can make word boundaries easier to see once you know them; it is not required for every word. Japanese normally uses **no spaces between words**, unlike the spaced teaching diagrams below.

**Try it:** In <span lang="ja"><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby>はコーヒーを<ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby>みます。</span>, identify the katakana word, then the two short hiragana particles. They are <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span>, <span lang="ja">は</span> and <span lang="ja">を</span>.

## 2. A typical sentence ends with its main verb

English often has **subject → verb → object**: “I → drink → coffee.”

A simple Japanese statement commonly places the verb last. Here is a *teaching diagram*, with spaces added only to reveal the parts:

<div class="foundation-order" role="group" aria-label="Comparing English and Japanese order">
  <div><span>English: subject → verb → object</span><strong>I → drink → coffee.</strong></div>
  <div><span>Japanese: topic → object → verb</span><strong lang="ja">わたしは → コーヒーを → のみます。</strong></div>
</div>

In normal Japanese writing: <span lang="ja">わたしはコーヒーをのみます。</span>

The translation is “I drink coffee,” not “I coffee drink” in natural English. Japanese sentence order and English word order do not match word-for-word.

Two more everyday examples, with the same shape:

| Japanese | Reading support | Natural English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">わたしはパンをたべます。</span> | *watashi wa pan o tabemasu* | I eat bread. |
| <span lang="ja">せんせいはひらがなをかきます。</span> | *sensei wa hiragana o kakimasu* | The teacher writes hiragana. |

The action words <span lang="ja">のみます</span> (drink), <span lang="ja">たべます</span> (eat), and <span lang="ja">かきます</span> (write) are polite **non-past** forms. They can describe habits or future actions depending on context. For this introduction, the English present tense makes the examples easy to compare.

**Notice the limit:** “Japanese is SOV” is a helpful *starting pattern*, not a law that every sentence has a stated subject, object and verb. Some sentences have no object; Japanese often omits an understood subject or topic; word order can vary for emphasis and discourse. We will learn those patterns progressively.

## 3. は marks a topic; を marks a direct object

In <span lang="ja">わたしはコーヒーをのみます。</span>, the word <span lang="ja">わたし</span> introduces the **topic**: “as for me.” The small <span lang="ja">は</span> marks that topic. It is written with the hiragana normally read *ha*, but **as a particle here it is pronounced *wa***.

<span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> is what is drunk: the **direct object** of the action. The particle <span lang="ja">を</span> follows it; in contemporary standard Japanese it is normally pronounced *o*.

<div class="foundation-scenario" role="group" aria-label="Sentence roles in context">
  <span class="foundation-scenario__caption">A café exchange</span>
  <div><strong lang="ja">コーヒーをのみます。</strong><span>(I / someone) drinks coffee. The topic is understood from context.</span></div>
  <div><strong lang="ja">わたしはおちゃをのみます。</strong><span>As for me, I drink tea.</span></div>
</div>

**A topic is not automatically the grammatical subject.** In our simple “I drink coffee” example the same person fills both roles, but this is not true in every Japanese sentence. The particle <span lang="ja">を</span> is an object marker here, not a word meaning “coffee” or “drink.” Do not translate particles as if each had one fixed English word.

A very useful contrast:

- <span lang="ja">コーヒーをのみます。</span> — “(I / someone) drink(s) coffee.” The drink is the object.
- <span lang="ja">コーヒーはのみます。</span> — “As for coffee, (I / someone) drink(s) it.” Here coffee is the topic, perhaps contrasting it with another drink.

**Both are possible Japanese sentences**, but they do not convey exactly the same focus. Avoid the mistaken rule that <span lang="ja">は</span> is simply a substitute for <span lang="ja">を</span>.

## 4. Polite です and polite action verbs do different jobs

Not every sentence has an action verb and object. Compare these two:

<div class="foundation-example" role="group" aria-label="Noun sentence compared with action sentence">
  <p lang="ja">これはほんです。</p>
  <p>This is a book. (<span lang="ja">これ</span> = this; <span lang="ja">ほん</span> = book; <span lang="ja">です</span> is a polite ending here.)</p>
</div>

<div class="foundation-example" role="group" aria-label="Action verb sentence">
  <p lang="ja">わたしはほんをよみます。</p>
  <p>I read a book. (<span lang="ja">よみます</span> = read, polite non-past.)</p>
</div>

<span lang="ja">です</span> can finish a polite noun-predicate sentence, but it is **not** a universal ending to add after every verb. In particular, <span lang="ja">のみますです</span> is **not** the normal way to say “drink” politely; <span lang="ja">のみます</span> is already polite.

The small <span lang="ja">は</span> in <span lang="ja">これはほんです。</span> marks the topic “this”; there is no <span lang="ja">を</span> because this is not a “do something to an object” sentence. This is why “every Japanese sentence is subject–object–verb” is too strong.

## 5. Compare common mistakes without memorizing false rules

1. **Wrong: every は sounds like *ha*.** When <span lang="ja">は</span> is the topic particle, it is pronounced *wa*. In an ordinary word such as <span lang="ja">はな</span> (flower), it is pronounced *ha*.
2. **Wrong: romaji is English.** *Watashi wa kōhī o nomimasu* represents Japanese sounds in Latin letters; “I drink coffee” is an English translation.
3. **Wrong: katakana words cannot be objects.** <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> is written in katakana and can be followed by <span lang="ja">を</span> like any other suitable noun.
4. **Wrong: all Japanese sentences must include “I.”** <span lang="ja">コーヒーをのみます。</span> is normal when the person is understood.
5. **Wrong: です always follows ます.** <span lang="ja">よみますです</span> is not the usual polite form. Use <span lang="ja">よみます</span> by itself.
6. **Too strong: Japanese word order never varies.** Verb-final is the default pattern in many ordinary clauses, but the topic can be omitted, and other elements can be reordered for context and emphasis.

**Guided check:** A classmate points at <span lang="ja">わたしはパンをたべます。</span> and asks “What is being eaten?” Look immediately before <span lang="ja">を</span>: <span lang="ja">パン</span> (bread). To find the action, look at the end: <span lang="ja">たべます</span> (eat).

## 6. Build a sentence for a real situation

Imagine a teacher asks what you drink at the café. Use this simple pattern:

**Topic + は → object + を → polite verb**

- Topic: <span lang="ja">わたし</span> (I)
- Object: <span lang="ja">おちゃ</span> (tea)
- Verb: <span lang="ja">のみます</span> (drink)

Together: <span lang="ja">わたしはおちゃをのみます。</span> — “I drink tea.”

Try changing only the object to <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span>. The result is <span lang="ja">わたしはコーヒーをのみます。</span> You do not change the particles because the roles have not changed. If the context already establishes who is speaking, <span lang="ja">コーヒーをのみます。</span> is also natural.

**Optional typing rehearsal:** On a Japanese keyboard, try entering <span lang="ja">わたしはおちゃをのみます。</span>. If you do not have an IME yet, write the words in romaji and compare them to the example; you will study Japanese typing tools later. Do not judge pronunciation from typing alone.

## 7. Review and what comes next

You can now explain that **kanji often write lexical stems, katakana often writes loanwords, and hiragana often writes particles and endings**, without claiming that each script has only one use. You can spot <span lang="ja">は</span> as a topic marker, <span lang="ja">を</span> as a direct-object marker, and the final verb in a simple sentence. You also know that <span lang="ja">です</span> and <span lang="ja">〜ます</span> are not interchangeable.

Use the interactive practice below to apply these distinctions. It tests **written grammar recognition and sentence function**, not audio comprehension, fluent speaking or kanji mastery. Lesson 04 will focus on recognizing and typing the written forms themselves.
