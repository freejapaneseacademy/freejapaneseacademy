## Your listening mission

You already learned to **read** a pronunciation map. Now put the chart away for a moment. You will hear two short, original Japanese conversations performed by a man and a woman. In both, someone asks about a photograph and receives an answer. Your job is to understand the *spoken meaning* and notice how a tiny difference in vowel duration can distinguish one word from another.

**Today's Can-do:** follow a simple spoken question and correction, tell a short vowel from a longer one when context helps, and explain what you actually heard. This is a listening task, **not** a test of recognizing printed kana, spelling, or identifying which writing system someone used.

The two tracks below are separate situations. Each has its own questions. Japanese transcripts and translations are deliberately hidden at first. You can reveal them for accessible assisted practice, or after submitting your answers. If you reveal a transcript before submitting, your work remains useful but is **not saved as a blind listening score**.

## 1. What a Japanese vowel sounds like

Japanese has five basic vowel qualities, conventionally called *a, i, u, e, o*. These labels are temporary listening aids, not English pronunciations. A useful first approximation is to keep each vowel **steady**, without the sliding ending common in some English accents.

| Vowel | Mouth and tongue cue | Common English-speaker trap |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *a* | Open, relaxed jaw; clear, steady sound | Turning it into an English “ay” diphthong |
| *i* | Tongue high and forward; lips relatively spread | Adding an extra glide at the end |
| *u* | Tongue high and back-ish, often with less lip rounding than English “oo” | Exaggerating rounded lips |
| *e* | Mid-front tongue position; a single stable sound | Turning it into English “ay” |
| *o* | Mid-back tongue position; keep it steady | Adding an English “oh-oo” glide |

These are approximate beginner cues. Actual Japanese vowels vary with speaker and neighboring sounds. You cannot diagnose your own pronunciation from a written table; **listen to the actual recordings** and compare your perception with the transcripts afterward.

## 2. Length changes the rhythm, sometimes the meaning

A **mora** is a unit of Japanese timing. An ordinary kana such as <span lang="ja">か</span> generally occupies one mora. Holding its vowel for an additional mora makes a longer sound. To feel the distinction without memorizing any answer to the coming conversations, try these **practice sounds, not vocabulary words**:

- <span lang="ja">か</span> — one rhythmic beat: *ka*.
- <span lang="ja">かあ</span> — two beats: *ka-a*, with a continuously held *a* sound rather than a pause or a new consonant.
- <span lang="ja">き</span> — one beat: *ki*.
- <span lang="ja">きい</span> — two beats: *ki-i*, keeping the *i* vowel steady.

Tap gently once for each written timing unit. Do **not** insert an English-style stress accent on the second beat. The longer vowel is not necessarily twice as loud, and a faster speaker can still preserve a meaningful length contrast. Some words also contain the nasal mora <span lang="ja">ん</span>, which counts as one beat of its own.

**Important:** spelling is useful for checking the rhythm later, but a blind listening question must be answered from what is audible. You will not be asked which kana were printed.

## 3. A three-pass listening strategy

**Pass 1 — situation:** Play Track A at normal speed without reading anything else. Who asks a question? Does the second person agree or correct the first? Play Track B and listen for a family relationship. It is normal not to catch every word.

**Pass 2 — the short reply:** Listen again. A brief answer near the start can change the meaning of the whole exchange. Notice whether a vowel seems to be held for another beat. A long vowel is not just a louder vowel; listen for **duration**.

**Pass 3 — confirm the message:** Listen for the word or phrase that gives the actual answer. Use the speaker's follow-up to check your interpretation. A polite repetition often confirms what someone meant. Then attempt the questions without opening the transcript.

You may pause, seek, replay, change volume or choose 0.8× speed. Slowing audio is optional and can distort the rhythm slightly; listen at normal speed again before deciding. If a recording cannot load, do not guess from the title. Use assisted transcript practice instead, and try the audio another time.

## 4. Track A — A photograph of a place

A man is looking at a photograph with a woman. He asks what kind of place is shown. She corrects him and gives a little more detail. **Listen for the first question, the first response, and the place they finally agree on.** The scene description does not tell you the answer.

Before answering, silently ask: *Did I hear a positive answer or a correction?* If the short reply is unclear, replay just that part. A familiar-sounding word is not enough: decide what the **spoken response** means in context.

## 5. Track B — A family photograph

The speakers look at a family photograph. One person guesses who is in it; the other corrects the relationship and adds a brief description. **Listen for the contrast between the guess and the corrected relationship.** One vowel may be noticeably longer. The final sentence gives one more detail about the person.

Do not assume that two similar-sounding kinship terms mean the same thing. Japanese distinguishes some word pairs by vowel length, and the surrounding conversation helps you decide which word was intended.

## 6. Guided practice before the scored check

Try these *unscored listening actions* using the two players below:

1. Play Track A and count how many **different people** speak. The male and female voices alternate; use that change to locate the reply to the first question.
2. Replay the first reply. Decide whether the speaker accepts or corrects the guess. Do not look at the transcript yet.
3. Play Track B. Listen for the initial guess, then the man's correction. Hold both possibilities in mind until you hear the complete answer.
4. Replay the final sentence in Track B. Try to catch whether the speaker says something positive or negative about the person.

These prompts are not separate scored exercises and do not pretend to grade pronunciation. The seven interactive questions below are the independent check. Each has one best answer and a detailed explanation with the actual Japanese evidence **after** submission. You can start a new attempt for more practice.

## 7. After you check your answers

Open both transcripts and compare the spoken utterances with the Japanese text and English meanings. Look for the extra vowel beat in the contrast words and for the response that changes the first speaker's guess. Count the morae **only after listening**; written characters are a review aid, not the listening stimulus.

If you missed a question, replay the precise turn and say its meaning in your own English words. If you heard the right meaning but missed a long vowel, listen again at 1× speed and tap the timing. You can also shadow one short sentence **optionally**, but no microphone or pronunciation score is required.

### Common mistakes to watch for

**Guessing from the scene description.** A photo scene gives context, not its answer. The speakers may disagree about what is shown. Listen for the correction.

**Treating a long vowel as extra stress.** Japanese vowel length is a timing contrast. A held vowel does not have to sound louder or more forceful.

**Equating two near-sounding words.** A single additional mora can change a word's meaning. Listen for the entire phrase and its reply, not just the first syllable.

**Reading before listening.** Reading a transcript is valuable for accessibility and later study, but it is a different evidence source. The exercise marks that as assisted and does not save it as blind listening mastery.

**Treating a practice score as pronunciation certification.** This quiz checks your choices about spoken meaning, not your ability to pronounce Japanese. Synthetic audio passed automated media checks, while independent native Japanese pronunciation and naturalness review remain pending.
