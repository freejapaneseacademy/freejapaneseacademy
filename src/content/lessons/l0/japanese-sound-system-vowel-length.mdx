Imagine a beginner lesson with **Emi**, a Japanese-speaking tutor, and **Ren**, a learner. Ren can already recognize <span lang="ja">あ・い・う・え・お</span> and a few words from the previous lessons. Today Ren learns that **holding a vowel for another timing unit can change the word**, not merely make it sound more enthusiastic.

## 1. Keep the five vowels steady

Japanese is conventionally taught with five basic vowel categories. Each kana below is a full vowel symbol, not the name of an English letter.

| Kana | Temporary label | Practical articulation cue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">あ</span> | a | Open your jaw; keep the vowel steady rather than sliding toward English “ay.” |
| <span lang="ja">い</span> | i | Raise the front of the tongue; keep the lips gently spread. Avoid a trailing glide. |
| <span lang="ja">う</span> | u | Raise the back of the tongue; keep the lips relatively relaxed. Japanese /u/ is typically less rounded than English “oo.” |
| <span lang="ja">え</span> | e | Use a steady mid-front vowel. Do not automatically turn it into the two-part vowel in English “day.” |
| <span lang="ja">お</span> | o | Use a steady mid-back vowel with some lip rounding, without adding English “ow.” |

**A practical limit:** these are approximate mouth-position cues, not a promise of identical articulation in every speaker or phonetic environment. The sound written <span lang="ja">う</span> is especially unlike many English “oo” vowels. Written cues cannot verify your actual pronunciation; use real Japanese audio and informed feedback when it becomes available.

**Guided noticing:** look at <span lang="ja">か</span> (*ka*), <span lang="ja">き</span> (*ki*), and <span lang="ja">く</span> (*ku*). The initial consonant is similar, but the vowel changes. Compare <span lang="ja">かさ</span> (*kasa*, umbrella) and <span lang="ja">いす</span> (*isu*, chair). You are recognizing written vowel categories, not claiming to hear them yet.

## 2. What counts as a mora?

A **mora** is a Japanese rhythm or timing unit. It is useful to count a short word in units as you practise, although natural speech is not a metronome and individual morae do not always last exactly the same number of milliseconds.

For the simple kana patterns used here, each ordinary full-size kana generally represents one mora. A long vowel contributes **one additional mora**. The extra unit belongs to the vowel: it is not an extra consonant, a louder voice, or a pause between two separate words.

| Written word | Break into morae | Count | Meaning in this lesson |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">かさ</span> | <span lang="ja">か・さ</span> | 2 | umbrella |
| <span lang="ja">いえ</span> | <span lang="ja">い・え</span> | 2 | house |
| <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> | <span lang="ja">い・い・え</span> | 3 | no |
| <span lang="ja">あおい</span> | <span lang="ja">あ・お・い</span> | 3 | blue (adjective) |
| <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> | <span lang="ja">お・ば・さ・ん</span> | 4 | aunt in a family context |
| <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> | <span lang="ja">お・ば・あ・さ・ん</span> | 5 | grandmother in a family context |

**Why does <span lang="ja">ん</span> count here?** It occupies a mora of its own. Chapter 03 will examine the nasal mora and other special timing patterns in detail. For now, count the visible <span lang="ja">ん</span> in these two words without trying to master all its pronunciations.

**Not the same as English syllables:** <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> has five morae even though an English speaker might group the sounds into fewer broad syllables. In the examples above, the separators are **teaching marks**. Normal Japanese words do not include dots or spaces between their morae.

### A small but important distinction: vowel sequence versus long vowel

The sequence <span lang="ja">いえ</span> has **two different vowels**, i then e; it is not a held i. In <span lang="ja">いいえ</span>, the first two written <span lang="ja">い</span> together represent a longer i before e. Similarly, <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> has a long a in the middle, whereas <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> has a short a there.

Written long vowels are not always shown by simply doubling the same kana. For example, the long o in <span lang="ja">こうこう</span> (*high school*) is written with <span lang="ja">う</span> after <span lang="ja">こ</span>: <span lang="ja">こ・う・こ・う</span> is four morae. The spelling is not a command to pronounce two unrelated vowels with a gap. Other spellings and pronunciations exist; this is a **preview**, not a complete spelling rule. Do not infer a universal long-vowel spelling from a single example.

## 3. Length changes the meaning, not just the speed

Emi holds up the house card. Ren reads <span lang="ja">いえ</span> (*house*). When Emi asks a yes/no question, Ren might instead answer <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> (*no*). These are **different words with different functions**:

- <span lang="ja">いえ</span> is a noun. In a picture-label activity it names a house. In a full polite sentence, <span lang="ja">これは いえ です。</span> means “This is a house.”
- <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> is a response meaning “no.” In a simple exchange, <span lang="ja">いいえ、ちがいます。</span> means “No, that's not right / that's different.” It is not a house label.

The extra <span lang="ja">い</span> is therefore not an optional flourish. **Leaving it out can change the message.**

Now look at Emi's family chart. In this context, <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> means *aunt*, and <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> means *grandmother*. These words can also refer more generally to a middle-aged woman or an elderly woman, depending on context; do not treat the English family labels as their only possible meanings. The contrast being studied is the **short a versus long a** in the middle.

A second common family-chart contrast is <span lang="ja">おじさん</span> (*uncle*) versus <span lang="ja">おじいさん</span> (*grandfather*). Here the i is lengthened instead. You do not need to memorize both pairs as new vocabulary today; use them to notice how a vowel-length contrast can carry meaning.

## 4. Apply the contrast in a real situation

A Japanese sentence does not change its grammatical word order just because a vowel is long. The **meaning of the word within that sentence or response** changes. This connects to the topic and polite sentence endings you previewed in Chapter 01.

| Situation | Japanese | Natural English |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pointing to a house picture | <span lang="ja">これは いえ です。</span> | This is a house. |
| Correcting an answer politely | <span lang="ja">いいえ、ちがいます。</span> | No, that's not correct. |
| Reading an aunt's family-chart label | <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> | Aunt (in this chart). |
| Reading a grandmother's family-chart label | <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> | Grandmother (in this chart). |

In <span lang="ja">これは いえ です。</span>, <span lang="ja">は</span> marks the topic and is pronounced *wa* as a particle; <span lang="ja">です</span> makes the statement polite. You only need to recognize this frame. In <span lang="ja">いいえ、ちがいます。</span>, the first word is the negative reply. **Do not substitute <span lang="ja">いえ</span> for <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> in that reply.**

### Three common mistakes, and how to repair them

1. **Using English letter names.** The letter “o” in English can be pronounced with a glide. Japanese <span lang="ja">お</span> is a steadier target. Compare vowel *quality* rather than English spelling.
2. **Treating a long vowel as stress or loudness.** A long a is held for an additional timing unit; shouting the short vowel does not make it long. In written practice, preserve the extra kana in <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span>.
3. **Counting syllables instead of morae.** <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> is <span lang="ja">い・い・え</span>, three morae, not two merely because it can feel like two English-style syllables. Likewise, <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> has five morae including <span lang="ja">ん</span>.

## 7. Review: the essential takeaways

- Five basic vowel labels: <span lang="ja">あ・い・う・え・お</span>. Aim for steady vowel quality, not English letter names.
- A mora is a timing unit. A long vowel contributes an extra mora, though real speech is not perfectly equal-timed.
- <span lang="ja">いえ</span> (house) and <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> (no) differ in both length and function.
- <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> and <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> differ in vowel length and, in a family chart, in meaning.
- Written recognition is preparation for listening, not proof of hearing or speaking accuracy.
