## A sign at the station kiosk

**Chapter Can-do:** Read and write katakana vowels and the K, S and T rows. Today you will use the *setting* to make sense of your first katakana labels. This is a written-reading lesson: no recording or audio is required.

At a small station kiosk, **Mika** is a customer looking for a drink and a snack. **Ren** works at the counter and points to the display. A kiosk sells small items; the labels below are **original teaching examples**, not a photograph or a claim about any actual shop.

| Person | Role | Purpose |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mika | Customer | Find a drink and a snack |
| Ren | Clerk | Show what is on the display |

### Display card

| Label on the kiosk | Meaning | Reading clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">アイス</span> | ice / ice cream, depending on context | ア・イ・ス |
| <span lang="ja">おかし</span> | sweets / snacks (hiragana here) | contrast: not all Japanese signs use katakana |
| <span lang="ja">カカオ</span> | cacao | カ・カ・オ |
| <span lang="ja">ココア</span> | cocoa | コ・コ・ア |
| <span lang="ja">テキスト</span> | textbook / text | テ・キ・ス・ト |

**Scene:** Mika sees <span lang="ja">ココア</span> on the drink display and <span lang="ja">おかし</span> on a snack shelf. Ren points to the two places. The sign's purpose is to **identify items for sale**, not to give a timetable or a train-platform number. We have deliberately limited the displayed katakana to the symbols introduced in this chapter; the hiragana snack label is a comparison.

## First symbols: look carefully

The five vowels are <span lang="ja">ア イ ウ エ オ</span> (a, i, u, e, o). The K row is <span lang="ja">カ キ ク ケ コ</span>; the S row is <span lang="ja">サ シ ス セ ソ</span>; the T row is <span lang="ja">タ チ ツ テ ト</span>. In Japanese, シ is read *shi*, チ *chi* and ツ *tsu*; these are conventional romanization hints, not extra letters in the katakana.

Katakana and hiragana can represent corresponding sounds but use **different shapes**: <span lang="ja">ア</span> and <span lang="ja">あ</span> both represent *a*; <span lang="ja">カ</span> and <span lang="ja">か</span> both represent *ka*. Do not confuse the scripts because the sounds match. Japanese frequently uses katakana for loanwords, sound effects and some names, but **not every katakana word is English**, and not every shop item must be written in katakana.

### Read the sign in three passes

1. Find the drink label <span lang="ja">ココア</span>. Trace the repeated <span lang="ja">コ</span> visually: it appears twice.
2. Find <span lang="ja">アイス</span> and point to its final <span lang="ja">ス</span>. Compare its shape with hiragana <span lang="ja">す</span>.
3. Find <span lang="ja">テキスト</span>. The final <span lang="ja">ト</span> belongs to the T row.
4. Find <span lang="ja">おかし</span>. Why does this label look different? It is written in hiragana.
5. Explain in English what Mika is trying to do and how the sign helps.

**Meaning caution:** <span lang="ja">アイス</span> can refer to ice or ice cream in context. Without more information on this short label, do not claim it necessarily means a particular dessert. <span lang="ja">ココア</span> is cocoa, not coffee; <span lang="ja">カカオ</span> refers to cacao.
