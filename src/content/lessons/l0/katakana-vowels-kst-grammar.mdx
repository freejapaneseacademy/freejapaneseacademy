## A label at the stationery counter

**Chapter Can-do:** Read and write katakana vowels and the K, S and T rows. This lesson teaches how to **interpret the shapes** rather than how to build Japanese sentences. Imagine a small stationery counter displaying cards with katakana symbols. A visitor is trying to tell apart similar-looking cards before copying them onto a practice sheet.

Katakana is one of Japanese's two phonographic kana scripts. The shapes are different from hiragana, but each ordinary kana represents a corresponding sound unit. In this chapter we work with the five vowels and the K, S and T families. You do **not** need to learn an entire loanword before identifying its individual kana.

## The shapes you can read

| Family | Symbols | Readings |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Vowels | <span lang="ja">ア イ ウ エ オ</span> | a, i, u, e, o |
| K | <span lang="ja">カ キ ク ケ コ</span> | ka, ki, ku, ke, ko |
| S | <span lang="ja">サ シ ス セ ソ</span> | sa, shi, su, se, so |
| T | <span lang="ja">タ チ ツ テ ト</span> | ta, chi, tsu, te, to |

**Sound warning:** The readings *shi*, *chi* and *tsu* are conventional English-friendly approximations, not evidence of pronunciation mastery. The T row is not a simple ta–ti–tu–te–to sequence in standard Japanese. The recorded sound practice belongs to Lesson 06.

## Spot the features, not just the overall silhouette

- <span lang="ja">ク</span> (*ku*) has two main strokes; <span lang="ja">ケ</span> (*ke*) has an additional vertical stroke. Do not confuse a longer-looking stroke with an extra stroke.
- <span lang="ja">シ</span> (*shi*) and <span lang="ja">ツ</span> (*tsu*) each have three marks. In a common printed style, the two short marks of **シ** align more vertically, and the long stroke sweeps upward from the lower left. In **ツ**, the two short marks sit more horizontally, and the long stroke descends from near the top right. Fonts and handwriting vary, so use the whole arrangement.
- <span lang="ja">ソ</span> (*so*) is a different character from <span lang="ja">ン</span> (*n*). **ン** is outside today's assigned rows; recognize that it exists, but you are not assessed on producing it yet.
- <span lang="ja">チ</span> (*chi*) and <span lang="ja">テ</span> (*te*) share a horizontal line near the top, but **チ** includes a distinctive curved lower stroke. Look at the complete form.

## Original card labels

A teacher places four **single-symbol** cards and three **practice strings** on a table. These are decoding aids, not claims that every string is a common Japanese word.

| Display | What to read | Evidence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">ア</span> | a | vowel row, first symbol |
| <span lang="ja">キ</span> | ki | K row, second symbol |
| <span lang="ja">シ</span> | shi | S row, second symbol |
| <span lang="ja">ツ</span> | tsu | T row, third symbol |
| <span lang="ja">カキ</span> | ka-ki | two K-row symbols |
| <span lang="ja">スシ</span> | su-shi | two S-row symbols; also the ordinary katakana spelling of sushi |
| <span lang="ja">テキ</span> | te-ki | T-row then K-row; a practice string here |

Katakana can appear in loanwords, names, emphasis, and labels; **not every katakana sequence is a loanword**. The displayed string <span lang="ja">テキ</span> is only a decoding exercise in this setting. Japanese spelling does not normally insert a hyphen between kana.

## Reason about a mix-up

If the visitor asks for the card <span lang="ja">ク</span> and the assistant points to <span lang="ja">ケ</span>, the mistake is **shape recognition**, not a change of grammatical particle or word order. Counting and locating strokes is more reliable than remembering only that both symbols have angled lines. In the same way, <span lang="ja">シ</span> and <span lang="ja">ツ</span> must be distinguished by stroke arrangement rather than by guessing from context.

**Try it without romaji:** Cover the readings in the table. Locate the S-row card that says *shi*, the T-row card that says *tsu*, and the K-row card that says *ke*. Then check the shape features. These are written recognition tasks; no audio or microphone is needed.
