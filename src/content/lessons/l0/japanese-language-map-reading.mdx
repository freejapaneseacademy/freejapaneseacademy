## A reading task, not a character-memory test

Imagine you are joining your first Japanese class. Your teacher gives you a one-page **script-identification chart** with examples from the classroom. You need to answer questions such as “Which example uses katakana?”, “Where is the reading guide?” and “What exact part of the chart proves that?” You are allowed to look back at the chart as often as you need. That is what real **reading for explicit information** involves.

**Today's Can-do:** read an original chart, find specified symbols and stated details, and **cite the row and exact written evidence**. You are not expected to recognize new kanji without help or to listen to audio. The chart below is the reading passage for this lesson; its row labels make it possible to check your evidence precisely.

### 1. Three moves that make reading reliable

1. **Scan** for a distinctive symbol or clue. If the question mentions a long-vowel mark, look for <span lang="ja">ー</span>, not merely any katakana.
2. **Locate** the row, then read the entire note. The example <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> is written in katakana, and the note explains what its two length marks do.
3. **Cite** evidence: name the row and quote the smallest relevant Japanese text or note. “Row C — <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> contains <span lang="ja">ー</span>” is stronger than “I think it looks like katakana.”

This is **open-chart practice**, not a closed-book memorization quiz. Looking back is encouraged; a correct answer without evidence is less useful than one you can justify.

### 2. What to notice before opening the chart

The teacher's chart includes familiar examples from lessons 01–04. Read these contrasts first:

- <span lang="ja">あ</span> and <span lang="ja">ア</span> are **different characters** representing the same vowel, *a*. The first is hiragana, the second katakana.
- <span lang="ja">さくら</span> (*sakura*) means “cherry blossom.” The Latin spelling *sakura* represents the **Japanese reading**, not the English translation. Do not confuse a romanized reading with a definition.
- <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> (*kōhī*) means “coffee.” Its <span lang="ja">ー</span> marks lengthen the preceding vowels; they are not hyphens or separate words.
- <span lang="ja"><ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby></span> (*yama*) means “mountain.” The large <span lang="ja">山</span> is kanji; the small <span lang="ja">やま</span> is **furigana**, a kana reading aid. The English meaning is *mountain*, not *yama*.
- <span lang="ja"><ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby></span> (*sensei*) means “teacher.” Again, the small kana supply a reading; they are not an English translation.

**Reading convention:** the chart uses a space in <span lang="ja">もういちど おねがいします</span> to help beginners see the two parts. Ordinary Japanese writing often omits spaces. The expression politely requests “One more time, please”; it is not a literal one-to-one translation of every written part.

### 3. Read the teacher's chart

The interactive chart below is the **original reading passage**. It has nine labeled rows (A–I), a Japanese example, a writing-system category, and a short teacher's note. The notes explicitly provide every fact you need; no hidden vocabulary or outside dictionary is required. On narrow screens, scroll **inside the chart** to read the final columns.

First, find these without revealing any answers:

1. A row with **two kana scripts** representing one vowel.
2. A row with **kanji and small kana above it**.
3. A row with **Latin letters**, even though the word is Japanese.
4. A row containing a **polite request** that a learner might say to a teacher.

If you can locate those rows, try the independent questions immediately below the chart. If not, use the hints in the next section and try again.

### 4. Work through two evidence examples

**Worked example A — identifying a symbol.** Question: “Where is the vowel-length mark?” Search the examples for <span lang="ja">ー</span>. The row with <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> includes the mark twice. The evidence is the written form itself **and** the teacher's note about extending the preceding vowel. Do not point to <span lang="ja">ヒ</span>; that is a katakana character, not the length mark.

**Worked example B — distinguishing reading from meaning.** Question: “Is *yama* an English translation?” No. The row containing <span lang="ja"><ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby></span> identifies <span lang="ja">やま</span> as the reading and *mountain* as the meaning. Quote both labels when explaining the difference. Similarly, *sakura* is the romaji reading of <span lang="ja">さくら</span>, while “cherry blossom” is the English meaning.

**A tempting wrong approach:** choosing a row only because a word looks familiar. For example, <span lang="ja">ひらがな</span> is the **name** of a script written in hiragana; <span lang="ja">さくら</span> is an ordinary **word** written in hiragana. Both contain hiragana, but the chart assigns them different purposes. Read the row note to determine what the question asks.

### 5. Three guided comparisons

**Comparison 1 — same sound, different writing:** In the pair <span lang="ja">あ / ア</span>, identify the hiragana and the katakana separately. Both cue *a*. Evidence should mention **both characters**, not merely the row label.

**Comparison 2 — same word, different representation:** <span lang="ja">さくら</span> is kana; *sakura* is romaji. Both can represent the Japanese word, but neither is the English translation “cherry blossom.” A chart question about **Latin letters** needs the romaji row, not the kana row.

**Comparison 3 — kanji with help:** In <span lang="ja"><ruby>先生<rt>せんせい</rt></ruby></span>, the small kana are a reading aid for the kanji. You can answer a chart question about that relationship **without memorizing** the kanji or claiming to have mastered it.

### 6. How to handle a tricky question

Suppose a classmate says: “The chart says <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> means coffee, so it must be written in English.” The **meaning** is given in English, but the **word** in the Japanese column is written in katakana. Cite the Japanese example and the script category separately. A translation does not change the writing system of the original text.

Another classmate says: “Romaji is an English translation because it uses English letters.” Correct this using the chart: romaji uses **Latin letters to represent Japanese readings**. The English definition is a different field. A Latin-letter spelling such as *sakura* is still Japanese in romanized form.

### 8. Review and next step

You can now scan a small chart for Japanese symbols, read the teacher's explicit notes, distinguish **script**, **reading**, and **English meaning**, and support an answer by citing a precise row. That is the L05 reading objective. It does **not** measure listening comprehension, which belongs to L06 and requires genuine, licensed, playable audio.
