## From hearing a vowel to writing a clear message

Imagine Emi is preparing picture cards for a beginner Japanese class. Ren will use them to identify an umbrella and a house, and to distinguish an aunt from a grandmother in a fictional family chart. The cards need **accurate Japanese writing**. A single missing vowel kana can turn a word into another expression.

**Chapter Can-do:** distinguish the five Japanese vowel qualities and the timing of morae. In this **L07 writing/typing step**, your evidence is the Japanese characters and short responses you produce. The previous listening lesson asked what you could understand by ear; this one asks whether you can **write** the intended word and explain its timing. A correct written answer does not prove you can hear or pronounce it accurately.

By the end, you will produce the five hiragana vowel forms, write two meaningful short/long contrasts, construct two polite classroom lines, and compare your own draft with a four-criterion rubric. You may use a Japanese IME or the on-screen kana palette. Your open composition is not machine-graded.

## 1. Retrieve five vowels as actual Japanese characters

The five Japanese vowel categories are conventionally labeled *a, i, u, e, o*. These Latin letters are beginner cues, not Japanese written answers. Try covering the hiragana column and writing each character before looking.

| Temporary vowel cue | Write in hiragana | Katakana partner (recognition only) | Writing check |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a | <span lang="ja">あ</span> | <span lang="ja">ア</span> | An open vowel; do not enter Latin a. |
| i | <span lang="ja">い</span> | <span lang="ja">イ</span> | Two visible strokes in common printed forms. |
| u | <span lang="ja">う</span> | <span lang="ja">ウ</span> | Do not substitute English “oo” spelling. |
| e | <span lang="ja">え</span> | <span lang="ja">エ</span> | Distinguish え from い. |
| o | <span lang="ja">お</span> | <span lang="ja">オ</span> | Distinguish お from あ. |

**First rehearsal:** look at <span lang="ja">あ・い・う・え・お</span>, hide the row, and type the sequence. Compare one glyph at a time. A romaji-input Japanese IME commonly converts *a* to <span lang="ja">あ</span> when hiragana mode is active; check the final displayed character before submitting. Device input methods differ. The workshop palette below can insert the characters if you do not have an IME.

**Articulation connection:** each written vowel represents one of the five vowel categories, which you studied using approximate mouth-position cues. A written symbol does not tell us whether your spoken vowel was steady or English-like. If you want to hear the categories in context, return to the real conversations in Lesson 06.

## 2. A mora is a timing unit, not an English letter

An ordinary kana such as <span lang="ja">か</span> (*ka*) normally counts as **one mora**, even though its romaji cue has two Latin letters. A long vowel adds another mora. In these beginner examples, a second written vowel kana preserves that extra timing unit.

| Word or reply | Break into morae | Count | Meaning in this classroom |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">かさ</span> | <span lang="ja">か・さ</span> | 2 | umbrella |
| <span lang="ja">いえ</span> | <span lang="ja">い・え</span> | 2 | house |
| <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> | <span lang="ja">い・い・え</span> | 3 | no, a reply |
| <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> | <span lang="ja">お・ば・さ・ん</span> | 4 | aunt, in a family chart |
| <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> | <span lang="ja">お・ば・あ・さ・ん</span> | 5 | grandmother, in the same chart |

The dots are **study separators**, not punctuation to type inside a normal Japanese word. <span lang="ja">ん</span> is a mora in its own right, so it counts in both family words. The additional <span lang="ja">あ</span> in <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> represents a held a vowel, not a second person or a pause. In natural speech, morae are not mechanically equal in duration; the contrast is still meaningful.

**Important spelling limit:** Japanese long vowels are not always spelled by doubling the same kana. The familiar example <span lang="ja">こうこう</span> (high school) uses <span lang="ja">う</span> after <span lang="ja">こ</span> for a long o. Do not invent spellings by doubling letters blindly. Today's scored items use only the explicitly taught words above.

## 3. Write the right word for the situation

The following two pairs are not interchangeable. They illustrate why **meaning, context and timing** must all guide your writing.

<div class="foundation-example" role="group" aria-label="House card example">
  <p lang="ja">これはいえです。</p>
  <p>*Kore wa ie desu.* — “This is a house.” Emi points to a house picture. The noun <span lang="ja">いえ</span> has two morae.</p>
</div>

<div class="foundation-example" role="group" aria-label="Polite correction example">
  <p lang="ja">いいえ、これはいえです。</p>
  <p>*Iie, kore wa ie desu.* — “No, this is a house.” Ren had mistaken the picture for a chair. The opening <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> is the three-mora negative reply, while the later <span lang="ja">いえ</span> is the two-mora noun.</p>
</div>

The frame <span lang="ja">これは…です</span> is a polite way to identify something you are pointing at. In this frame, the particle <span lang="ja">は</span> is pronounced *wa*. Japanese normally writes the sentence without the instructional spaces sometimes used in earlier lessons. **Do not change the negative reply into <span lang="ja">いえ</span>**; that would say “house,” not “no.”

Now imagine a family chart, not an actual person's private data:

<div class="foundation-example" role="group" aria-label="Family chart vocabulary comparison">
  <p lang="ja">おばさん　／　おばあさん</p>
  <p>In this fictional family context, the first means “aunt” (four morae) and the second means “grandmother” (five morae). Outside a family chart, they can also refer more generally to a middle-aged woman and an elderly woman. Do not assume these are their only meanings.</p>
</div>

The written contrast is a **short versus long a**. It is not a change in loudness. Do not insert a space or punctuation between <span lang="ja">ば</span> and <span lang="ja">あ</span> in <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span>.

## 5. Mistakes to diagnose and repair

**Replacing kana with romaji.** Typing *ie* may be an IME input sequence, but the submitted Japanese noun must be <span lang="ja">いえ</span>. A Latin-letter string is not a hiragana answer.

**Deleting a long-vowel unit.** <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> and <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> are different words. Do not let autocorrect or a rushed keyboard entry remove <span lang="ja">あ</span>.

**Confusing a negative reply with a house.** <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> and <span lang="ja">いえ</span> share characters but have different meanings and functions. A picture card needs the noun; a disagreement needs the negative reply.

**Counting English syllables instead of Japanese morae.** <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> has five morae, including <span lang="ja">ん</span>, regardless of how many broad English-style syllables you perceive.

**Treating correct writing as correct pronunciation.** A perfect typing score cannot verify vowel quality, pitch accent or natural prosody. Use real audio and appropriate human feedback for that separate skill.

## 6. Your independent writing task

In the workshop below, complete three stages:

1. **Constrained production:** type ten hiragana entries, from single vowels to complete words. The exact targets have one unambiguous answer in the stated context. Feedback explains mistakes; retries create separate browser-local attempts.
2. **Guided choice:** select the appropriate written expression for four fictional situations. Check and revise. This stage is formative and does not save a score.
3. **Independent composition:** write one polite line identifying an umbrella and one polite negative reply correcting a house picture. Then write an English note explaining both vowel-length pairs with mora counts. Compare your response to a model and tick a self-review rubric.

A good independent response is understandable in the given situation, uses the correct kana, preserves meaningful long vowels, and explains why the two contrasts change the message. Other natural polite responses may be valid; an exact-match quiz is **not** a fair automatic grader for free composition. Your draft stays in page memory unless you explicitly download it.
