## A new scene: a five-vowel practice table

Imagine a beginner-friendly language-exchange session. **Emi**, a Japanese-speaking tutor, puts a card with five large hiragana characters on the table. **Ren**, a new learner, wants to know why English spelling is not always a reliable guide to Japanese pronunciation. The card is not an exam. It is a way to notice the sounds and their rhythm before trying to understand natural speech.

**Your chapter Can-do:** distinguish Japanese **mora timing** and the **five vowel qualities**. This first lesson is the *context-and-meaning* step: understand who is speaking, what the practice is for, and what to notice on the written card. Later Chapter 02 lessons will build the actual listening and production skills. A text-only card cannot prove that you can hear or pronounce the sounds accurately.

### A short supported classroom exchange

The Japanese below is written in kana so you can focus on the sounds. The spaces are **learning aids**; ordinary Japanese writing usually omits these spaces. You do not need to memorize the grammar yet.

| Speaker | Japanese | Natural English meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Emi, tutor | <span lang="ja">これは にほんごの ぼいんです。</span> | “These are Japanese vowels.” |
| Emi | <span lang="ja">あ・い・う・え・お。</span> | “A, i, u, e, o.” (the five vowel labels) |
| Ren, learner | <span lang="ja">「あ」と「お」は おなじですか。</span> | “Are ‘a’ and ‘o’ the same?” |
| Emi | <span lang="ja">いいえ、ちがいます。</span> | “No, they are different.” |
| Ren | <span lang="ja">ゆっくり いってください。</span> | “Please say it slowly.” |

**Context clues:** <span lang="ja">にほんご</span> means *Japanese language*, <span lang="ja">ぼいん</span> means *vowel*, <span lang="ja">おなじ</span> means *the same*, and <span lang="ja">ちがいます</span> means *they differ*. <span lang="ja">ゆっくり いってください</span> is a polite request to say something slowly. These expressions make the exchange understandable; today's assessment does **not** demand that you produce the whole dialogue.

**Who does what?** Emi introduces and models the five-vowel card. Ren asks about a contrast and requests slower speech. The purpose is to *notice distinct vowel categories and timing*, not to master every character or the polite question pattern.

## 1. Five vowels: one stable target per card

Japanese is commonly described as having five basic vowel categories. The hiragana forms and temporary romaji labels are:

| Kana | Label | What to notice when learning the sound |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">あ</span> | a | Open the mouth, with relaxed lips. A steady open vowel, not English “ay.” |
| <span lang="ja">い</span> | i | Tongue high and toward the front, with relatively spread lips. Avoid adding an English-style ending glide. |
| <span lang="ja">う</span> | u | Tongue high and farther back; the lips are generally less rounded than for English “oo” in *food*. |
| <span lang="ja">え</span> | e | A steady mid-front vowel, roughly like the start of “eh,” not the two-part English vowel in *day*. |
| <span lang="ja">お</span> | o | A steady mid-back vowel, with some lip rounding. Avoid turning it into English “ow.” |

The roman letters are **labels**, not an exact English pronunciation key. Individual speakers and surrounding sounds affect articulation. In particular, Japanese <span lang="ja">う</span> is often described phonetically as a high, relatively unrounded or compressed vowel. Do not assume it is identical to an English “oo.”

**Guided noticing:** point to <span lang="ja">あ</span>, then <span lang="ja">お</span>. Both are vowels, but they are different categories. Point to <span lang="ja">い</span> and <span lang="ja">え</span>; both are made toward the front of the mouth, but the tongue is higher for <span lang="ja">い</span>. This is an introductory articulation map, not a sound-quality measurement.

## 2. A mora is a rhythm unit, not simply an English syllable

A **mora** is a small timing unit used to describe Japanese rhythm. The word *morae* is the plural. A simple vowel kana like <span lang="ja">あ</span> usually represents **one mora**. In our first written examples, each displayed vowel character represents a separate unit.

<div class="foundation-order" role="group" aria-label="Written mora segmentation examples">
  <div><span>One mora</span><strong lang="ja">あ → あ (1)</strong></div>
  <div><span>Two morae</span><strong lang="ja">いえ → い・え (2)</strong></div>
  <div><span>Three morae</span><strong lang="ja">あおい → あ・お・い (3)</strong></div>
  <div><span>Length preview</span><strong lang="ja">あ → ああ (1 → 2)</strong></div>
</div>

The word <span lang="ja">いえ</span> (*ie*) means “house.” It contains two vowel units, <span lang="ja">い</span> and <span lang="ja">え</span>. Don't collapse them into the English word *eye*, which has a different sound pattern. <span lang="ja">あおい</span> (*aoi*) means “blue” as an adjective: three written vowel units, <span lang="ja">あ・お・い</span>. You may already recognize <span lang="ja">さくら</span> (*sakura*, cherry blossom) from Chapter 01; its simple kana sequence can be segmented <span lang="ja">さ・く・ら</span>, three morae.

**Try it without a recording:** tap once for each separated kana in <span lang="ja">い・え</span>, then three times for <span lang="ja">あ・お・い</span>. Keep the taps reasonably even. This is a *written rhythm rehearsal*, not a substitute for hearing natural Japanese. Actual mora duration varies with context and speech rate; Japanese is not a mechanical metronome.

### Why vowel length matters

Compare the *written timing patterns* <span lang="ja">あ</span> and <span lang="ja">ああ</span>. The second has an extra vowel unit and can represent a longer sound in context. The example is about **duration**, not a claim that the two strings have identical meanings or that all long vowels use doubled <span lang="ja">あ</span>. Later lessons explain long-vowel spelling in actual words.

You may eventually meet contrasts such as <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> (“aunt; middle-aged woman”) and <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> (“grandmother”). The extra <span lang="ja">あ</span> matters to the word. Those words also contain <span lang="ja">ん</span>, a special mora studied later; **do not treat this as today's memorization or counting test**.

## 3. English spelling can hide Japanese distinctions

A common beginner mistake is to read Japanese vowel letters as if they were English letter names. The letter *a* in English can represent several different sounds; Japanese <span lang="ja">あ</span> is taught as a relatively stable vowel target. Similarly, the written pair <span lang="ja">あい</span> is <span lang="ja">あ・い</span>, two morae, even though English *eye* is often perceived as one syllable.

| You see | Avoid assuming | Better first observation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">いえ</span> | It is the English sound “eye.” | <span lang="ja">い・え</span> contains two separate Japanese vowel units. |
| <span lang="ja">う</span> | It must sound exactly like English “oo.” | The Japanese vowel has its own articulation and typically less lip rounding. |
| <span lang="ja">ああ</span> | An extra vowel cannot affect timing. | A repeated vowel can add a mora. |
| <span lang="ja">あおい</span> | Three written vowels are one quick English-style glide. | <span lang="ja">あ・お・い</span> has three written morae. |

**Another important distinction:** a *vowel quality* is which vowel you produce (for example, <span lang="ja">あ</span> versus <span lang="ja">お</span>). *Vowel length* is how long a vowel lasts. These are related but **different** features. The chapter will teach both progressively.

## 4. Guided mini-practice before the interactive check

Read each prompt, choose an answer in your mind, then compare with the explanation below.

**A.** Emi points to <span lang="ja">え</span>. Is its vowel label *e* or *i*? It is **e**; <span lang="ja">い</span> is the *i* card.

**B.** Ren sees <span lang="ja">いえ</span>. Should Ren tap once or twice? **Twice**, for <span lang="ja">い・え</span>. The English word *eye* is not a reliable model for this Japanese sequence.

**C.** Emi adds a second <span lang="ja">あ</span> to make <span lang="ja">ああ</span>. Does the written timing count stay the same? **No**: the exercise changes from one to two morae.

**D.** Ren answers every written question correctly. Can Emi conclude that Ren now hears all Japanese vowels accurately? **No**. Written recognition and acoustic discrimination are different skills. A real listening task must use genuine audio and suitable answer checks.
