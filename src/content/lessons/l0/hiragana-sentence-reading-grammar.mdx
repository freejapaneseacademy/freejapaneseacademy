## Follow the small clues in a sentence

**Chapter Can-do:** Decode short hiragana sentences without relying on romaji. You already know the words from Yui and Sora's library conversation. This lesson focuses on how particles and punctuation tell you what each sentence does. It is a **written grammar lesson**, not a listening or speaking assessment.

Japanese normally does **not** separate every word with spaces. The teaching examples below add spaces after some particles for beginners; those spaces are not part of standard spelling.

## Three useful particles

| Particle | Job in this dialogue | Example | Natural meaning |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">は</span> | Marks what the sentence is about (topic) | <span lang="ja">えほんは ここです。</span> | The picture books are here. |
| <span lang="ja">を</span> | Marks the thing affected by an action (object) | <span lang="ja">えほんを みます。</span> | I'll look at picture books. |
| <span lang="ja">か</span> | At the end of a polite statement, makes a question | <span lang="ja">えほんは ありますか。</span> | Do you have picture books? |

### Topic は is written *ha* but spoken *wa*

The hiragana <span lang="ja">は</span> usually represents *ha* in words, but **as the topic particle** it is pronounced *wa*. For example, in <span lang="ja">えほんは ここです。</span>, <span lang="ja">えほん</span> is the topic. Do not replace written <span lang="ja">は</span> with <span lang="ja">わ</span> when writing this particle. You do not need romaji to decode the sentence: recognize the particle's role.

### Object を is not the topic

Compare <span lang="ja">えほんは ここです。</span> with <span lang="ja">えほんを みます。</span>. The first tells us where the picture books are; the second says what someone looks at. The particle <span lang="ja">を</span> marks the object of <span lang="ja">みます</span>. In modern standard Japanese it is usually pronounced *o*. It does **not** mean “buy” or “borrow.”

### Question か follows the predicate

Compare <span lang="ja">あります。</span> (“There is / we have”) with <span lang="ja">ありますか。</span> (“Is there / do you have?”). The added <span lang="ja">か</span> signals a question. It belongs at the **end** of this polite question, not before <span lang="ja">あります</span>. English often uses a question mark, but Japanese can use <span lang="ja">。</span> after a question ending in <span lang="ja">か</span>; <span lang="ja">？</span> is also used in many contemporary contexts.

## Two punctuation signs

- <span lang="ja">。</span> is the Japanese full stop. It ends a sentence, including the polite question <span lang="ja">ありますか。</span> in this lesson.
- <span lang="ja">、</span> is a Japanese comma. In <span lang="ja">はい、あります。</span>, it separates the acknowledgment <span lang="ja">はい</span> from the rest of the reply; it is not a new word.

Punctuation helps you see boundaries, but it does not replace reading the particles. A comma does not turn a statement into a question.

## Re-read the six-line dialogue

| Speaker | Japanese | Sentence clue |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Yui | <span lang="ja">こんにちは。</span> | Full stop closes a greeting. |
| Yui | <span lang="ja">えほんは ありますか。</span> | は marks the topic; か asks about availability. |
| Sora | <span lang="ja">はい、あります。</span> | 、 separates “yes” from the response. |
| Sora | <span lang="ja">えほんは ここです。</span> | は marks the topic; ここ says “here.” |
| Yui | <span lang="ja">えほんを みます。</span> | を marks the thing Yui looks at. |
| Yui | <span lang="ja">ありがとうございます。</span> | Full stop closes a polite thanks. |

**Supported practice:** Cover the English explanations. First locate the <span lang="ja">か</span> that makes a question. Next find the <span lang="ja">を</span> after <span lang="ja">えほん</span>. Finally compare the two lines containing <span lang="ja">は</span>. What stays the same (topic) and what changes (predicate)?

**Common misuse:** <span lang="ja">えほんを ありますか。</span> is not the pattern taught here: <span lang="ja">あります</span> expresses existence or availability, not a direct action on a book. In this exchange, use <span lang="ja">えほんは ありますか。</span> for the availability question, and <span lang="ja">えほんを みます。</span> for the action of looking at books. Other Japanese constructions can use different particles in different contexts; these are not universal word-for-word English equivalents.
