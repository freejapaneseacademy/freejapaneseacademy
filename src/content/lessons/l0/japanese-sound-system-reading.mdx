## The task: read Emi's pronunciation map

Emi gives Ren an original classroom handout: a **pronunciation map** with eleven labeled rows. Each row provides written Japanese, a temporary reading cue or meaning, and a note about vowel quality or **mora** timing. A mora is a unit of Japanese rhythm; an ordinary kana such as <span lang="ja">か</span> normally takes one mora. Your job is to find information **in the map**, not to guess how an unheard recording sounds.

**Today's Can-do:** identify specified symbols and explicit details, then cite the row and the smallest relevant Japanese text or note. You may consult the map throughout the check. This is **written reading comprehension**, not the chapter's L06 listening test: actual vowel discrimination requires real playable audio.

## 1. How to read a map rather than guess

1. **Scan for the clue.** A question about the vowel *e* calls for <span lang="ja">え</span> or <span lang="ja">エ</span>; a question about a length mark calls for <span lang="ja">ー</span>.
2. **Read the whole row.** The Japanese column shows the spelling. The teacher's note explains its reading, meaning or timing. A Latin reading cue is not an English translation.
3. **Cite your evidence.** A strong response says: “Row I prints <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> as <span lang="ja">い・い・え</span>, three morae, and labels it ‘no.’” A row letter alone is less informative.

The chart appears directly below in an accessible, scrollable table. Each note is part of the reading passage; no dictionary, audio player or prior kanji knowledge is needed to answer its questions.

## 2. Five vowel categories and two scripts

Japanese has five basic vowel categories, often introduced with the temporary Latin cues *a, i, u, e, o*. These cues are **labels**, not a guarantee that English vowels sound identical. In the map, Rows A–E pair hiragana with katakana:

| Category | Hiragana | Katakana | Approximate articulation clue |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| a | <span lang="ja">あ</span> | <span lang="ja">ア</span> | Open the mouth for a steady vowel. |
| i | <span lang="ja">い</span> | <span lang="ja">イ</span> | Tongue high and toward the front. |
| u | <span lang="ja">う</span> | <span lang="ja">ウ</span> | High vowel, typically without strong English “oo” lip rounding. |
| e | <span lang="ja">え</span> | <span lang="ja">エ</span> | Mid front vowel; keep it distinct from i. |
| o | <span lang="ja">お</span> | <span lang="ja">オ</span> | Mid back vowel; do not add an English-style glide. |

These are beginner **written descriptions**, not recordings or precise individual pronunciation diagnoses. A kana shape can identify the intended category, but reading the chart does not prove that you can hear or pronounce it.

**Guided lookup A:** find the row that pairs <span lang="ja">う</span> with <span lang="ja">ウ</span>. The row's note says the vowel is usually produced with less lip rounding than English “oo.” Check the actual chart before continuing: the correct evidence is **Row C — う / ウ**.

**Guided lookup B:** <span lang="ja">え</span> and <span lang="ja">エ</span> are not the i-vowel pair. Which row shows them? **Row D**. Compare with **Row B — い / イ**. The shape and category must both match.

## 3. One written kana is not one English word

Row F lays out <span lang="ja">か・き・く・け・こ</span>, the five k-row hiragana. The temporary cues are *ka, ki, ku, ke, ko*. Each ordinary consonant–vowel kana normally occupies **one mora**, although its Latin cue uses two letters. The whole sequence is five morae when read as five separate syllables; it is not presented as a single vocabulary word.

Rows G–I show three useful classroom picture-card labels:

| Label | Reading units | Meaning in the scene |
| --- | --- | --- |
| <span lang="ja">かさ</span> | <span lang="ja">か・さ</span> (2) | umbrella |
| <span lang="ja">いえ</span> | <span lang="ja">い・え</span> (2) | house |
| <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> | <span lang="ja">い・い・え</span> (3) | no, as a reply |

Notice that <span lang="ja">いえ</span> (*ie*, house) and <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> (*iie*, no) are **different words**. Do not erase the extra <span lang="ja">い</span> or assume that every extra vowel is an optional spelling decoration. The extra unit matters.

**Guided comparison:** Ren points to a house, while Emi answers a yes/no question. Which row is the house label, and which is the reply? **H** is <span lang="ja">いえ</span> (house); **I** is <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> (no). Cite the spellings as well as the row letters.

## 4. Read length and the nasal mora carefully

Row J compares two words on a fictional family chart. <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> (aunt, in this scene) is <span lang="ja">お・ば・さ・ん</span> (four morae). <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> (grandmother, in this scene) is <span lang="ja">お・ば・あ・さ・ん</span> (five morae). The extra <span lang="ja">あ</span> lengthens the vowel. The final <span lang="ja">ん</span> counts as its own mora. These kinship terms also have broader uses outside the chart; here the fictional family labels fix their meanings.

Row K uses katakana: <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> (*kōhī*, coffee). Its written timing is <span lang="ja">コ・ー・ヒ・ー</span>, **four morae**. The mark <span lang="ja">ー</span> extends the preceding vowel; it is not an English hyphen, a separate word, or an additional consonant.

**Important exception to a simplistic rule:** long vowels are not always written by repeating the same kana. For example, <span lang="ja">こうこう</span> (*kōkō*, high school) uses <span lang="ja">う</span> in its conventional spelling. Read the actual word and chart instead of applying an invented “double every vowel” rule.

## 5. Common reading mistakes and how to correct them

**Mistake: treating romaji as English translation.** *Kasa* is a Latin-letter reading cue for <span lang="ja">かさ</span>; “umbrella” is its English meaning. The chart deliberately separates the Japanese example from its note.

**Mistake: counting Latin letters instead of morae.** The cue *ka* contains two Latin letters but <span lang="ja">か</span> normally occupies one mora. The five k-row symbols in Row F are five morae in sequence, not ten.

**Mistake: overlooking an extra symbol.** <span lang="ja">いえ</span> and <span lang="ja">いいえ</span> differ by one <span lang="ja">い</span>; <span lang="ja">おばさん</span> and <span lang="ja">おばあさん</span> differ by one <span lang="ja">あ</span>. Written differences can correspond to different meanings and timing.

**Mistake: claiming the map proves pronunciation.** A text description is not a recorded minimal pair. In L06 you will listen to real audio without initially seeing its transcript, then check what you heard.
