## First, imagine opening a Japanese menu

You have just arrived at a small café in Japan. The menu includes a familiar-looking word, a word written in flowing curves, and a short heading made of more complex characters. They are all Japanese, yet they don't look alike. **Why?** Because modern Japanese commonly uses *three* different writing systems together—and you may also encounter Japanese written using the Latin alphabet.

You don't need to read every character today. Your goal is to **recognize what kind of writing you're looking at and why it might be there.** That makes your first real character lesson much less mysterious.

These are **illustrations**, not vocabulary you must memorize in this orientation lesson. We will study sounds, words, and grammar systematically in later lessons.

## 1. Hiragana — the flexible foundation

<span class="foundation-script-sample" lang="ja">ひらがな　さくら　おちゃ</span>

**Hiragana** (ひらがな) consists of rounded, flowing-looking characters. Each basic character represents a Japanese sound unit called a *mora*. For now, you can think of it as a small rhythm unit. Unlike the English alphabet, hiragana characters are generally not single consonant letters.

Hiragana is used for many everyday Japanese words, grammar endings, and particles (small words that help show how the sentence works). Children start learning it early, and you'll use it throughout your Japanese studies.

- <span lang="ja">さくら</span> (*sakura*) — cherry blossom.
- <span lang="ja">おちゃ</span> (*ocha*) — tea.

**What to notice:** The characters look relatively curved. You don't have to know their pronunciation yet—recognizing the **category** comes first.

## 2. Katakana — another sound-based script

<span class="foundation-script-sample" lang="ja">カタカナ　コーヒー　テレビ</span>

**Katakana** (カタカナ) represents the same underlying Japanese sound system as hiragana, but its shapes tend to look straighter and more angular. It is often used for borrowed foreign words, many non-Japanese names, sound effects, and sometimes stylistic emphasis.

- <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> (*kōhī*) — coffee, a borrowed word.
- <span lang="ja">テレビ</span> (*terebi*) — television, another borrowed word.

**What to notice:** Katakana is not a separate spoken language. A Japanese sound may have a hiragana spelling and a katakana spelling. Different scripts help signal different writing functions.

**A common misunderstanding:** "Katakana is only for foreign words" is too absolute. Foreign borrowings are a very common use, but katakana can also be used for emphasis, sound effects, and other purposes.

## 3. Kanji — characters carrying meaning and readings

<span class="foundation-script-sample" lang="ja"><ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby>　<ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby>　<ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby></span>

**Kanji** (漢字) are characters historically derived from Chinese writing. They often give useful clues about meaning, and they are used to write many words and word parts. A kanji can have more than one reading depending on the word or context. We'll learn readings **through useful words**, not by guessing that every character has one fixed sound.

- <span lang="ja"><ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby></span> — Japan. The reading is shown in small characters above the kanji.
- <span lang="ja"><ruby>山<rt>やま</rt></ruby></span> — mountain.
- <span lang="ja"><ruby>人<rt>ひと</rt></ruby></span> — person.

The small reading text above kanji is called **furigana**. It is a learning aid, **not a fourth writing system**. Furigana will help you read examples while you build kanji knowledge.

**A common misunderstanding:** Kanji does not replace hiragana or katakana. Japanese regularly combines these writing systems in the same sentence.

## 4. Romaji — Japanese shown in Latin letters

<span class="foundation-script-sample">sakura　ocha　Nihon</span>

**Romaji** (ローマ字) means writing Japanese sounds using Latin letters like `a`, `k`, and `s`—the letters you're reading right now.

Compare:

| Romaji | Japanese writing | Meaning |
| --- | --- | --- |
| *sakura* | <span lang="ja">さくら</span> | cherry blossom |
| *ocha* | <span lang="ja">おちゃ</span> | tea |
| *Nihon* | <span lang="ja"><ruby>日本<rt>にほん</rt></ruby></span> | Japan |

Romaji can help absolute beginners and appear on signs, passports, maps, or brand names. But most Japanese text is **not written entirely in romaji**, so we will gradually move away from relying on it.

**Notice:** Romaji is a *way to represent Japanese with another alphabet*. It isn't another set of Japanese characters that you must memorize.

## 5. How three Japanese scripts fit together

A real sentence can contain more than one system. Consider this example:

<div class="foundation-example" role="group" aria-label="Japanese sentence with a mixture of kanji, hiragana and katakana">
  <p lang="ja"><span class="foundation-kanji"><ruby>私<rt>わたし</rt></ruby></span><span class="foundation-hira">は</span><span class="foundation-kata">コーヒー</span><span class="foundation-hira">を</span><span class="foundation-kanji"><ruby>飲<rt>の</rt></ruby></span><span class="foundation-hira">みます。</span></p>
  <p>"I drink coffee." (polite style)</p>
  <div class="foundation-legend"><span><i class="foundation-dot foundation-dot--hira"></i>Hiragana</span><span><i class="foundation-dot foundation-dot--kata"></i>Katakana</span><span><i class="foundation-dot foundation-dot--kanji"></i>Kanji</span></div>
</div>

The sentence mixes <strong>kanji</strong> for word parts, <strong>katakana</strong> for the borrowed word <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span>, and <strong>hiragana</strong> for particles and a verb ending. You **do not need to learn these words or grammar forms yet**. Just notice how all three scripts belong together.

**One extra detail:** The hiragana <span lang="ja">は</span> is pronounced *wa* when used as this sentence's topic particle. We'll learn the reason in a later lesson, so don't worry about it now.

## 6. A first peek at Japanese sentence order

English often uses the pattern **subject → verb → object**: "I **drink** tea."

In a basic Japanese sentence, the **verb often comes at the end**. Compare:

<div class="foundation-order">
  <div><span>English</span><strong>I → drink → tea.</strong></div>
  <div><span>Japanese (illustrative)</span><strong lang="ja">わたしは → おちゃを → のみます。</strong></div>
</div>

This means something like: "As for me → tea (the object) → drink." In regular Japanese writing those words are normally joined without English-style spaces: <span lang="ja">わたしはおちゃをのみます。</span>

Japanese often leaves out an obvious subject and has other word-order possibilities, so this is a **starting observation**, not an absolute rule. You'll learn the real sentence patterns later.

## 7. Four mistakes to avoid at the beginning

1. **"Hiragana and katakana make different languages."** They are two scripts used to write Japanese sounds.
2. **"One kanji always has one reading."** Readings can change with the word and context.
3. **"Furigana is another writing system."** It is small reading guidance, usually written in kana.
4. **"I can keep using romaji forever."** Romaji can help initially, but learning kana makes reading real Japanese possible.

## 8. Quick recap before practice

| If you notice… | Your best first guess |
| --- | --- |
| Curvy Japanese sound characters like <span lang="ja">さくら</span> | Hiragana |
| Angular characters like <span lang="ja">コーヒー</span> | Katakana |
| Meaning-associated characters like <span lang="ja">山</span> | Kanji |
| Latin letters like *sakura* | Romaji |

Your next task is to **identify the scripts and explain the difference**. You are not expected to write or pronounce a full kana chart yet. That will begin in our dedicated Hiragana module.
